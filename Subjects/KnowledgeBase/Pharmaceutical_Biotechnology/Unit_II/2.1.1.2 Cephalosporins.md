# 2.1.1.2 Cephalosporins

Cephalosporins are another major class of [[2.1.1 β-Lactam Antibiotics|β-lactam antibiotics]], structurally related to penicillins but often with a broader spectrum of activity and increased stability against many β-lactamases.

**Chemical Structure:**
Contain a β-lactam ring fused to a six-membered dihydrothiazine ring (instead of the five-membered thiazolidine ring in penicillins). Modifications at positions 3 and 7 of the core structure lead to different generations with varying properties.

**Generations and Examples:**
Cephalosporins are often grouped into generations based primarily on their spectrum of activity, particularly against Gram-negative bacteria.
-   **First Generation:** Good activity against Gram-positive cocci (like *Staphylococcus* and *Streptococcus*), moderate activity against some Gram-negatives. More susceptible to β-lactamases.
    -   *Examples:* Cefazolin, Cephalexin. Used for skin infections, surgical prophylaxis.
-   **Second Generation:** Increased activity against Gram-negatives compared to first-gen, retain good Gram-positive coverage. Some have activity against anaerobes. More resistant to some β-lactamases.
    -   *Examples:* Cefuroxime, Cefoxitin, Cefotetan. Used for respiratory infections, intra-abdominal infections.
-   **Third Generation:** Broad spectrum, significantly enhanced activity against Gram-negatives (including Enterobacteriaceae), often less active against Gram-positives than first-gen. Some cross the blood-brain barrier. More resistant to common β-lactamases.
    -   *Examples:* Ceftriaxone, Cefotaxime, Ceftazidime (good anti-pseudomonal activity). Used for serious infections like meningitis, pneumonia, sepsis, gonorrhea.
-   **Fourth Generation:** Extended spectrum, good activity against both Gram-positives and Gram-negatives (including *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*). More resistant to hydrolysis by chromosomal β-lactamases (e.g., AmpC).
    -   *Example:* Cefepime. Used for severe nosocomial infections.
-   **Fifth Generation (Advanced Generation):** Broad spectrum with activity against Methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA).
    -   *Example:* Ceftaroline. Used for complicated skin infections and community-acquired pneumonia, including cases involving MRSA.

**Mechanism of Action:**
Similar to penicillins, they inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to and inactivating Penicillin-Binding Proteins (PBPs), preventing peptidoglycan cross-linking. (See [[2.3.1 Interference with Cell Wall Synthesis]])

**Resistance Mechanisms:**
-   **β-Lactamase Production:** Hydrolysis of the β-lactam ring. Includes common penicillinases, cephalosporinases (like AmpC), and Extended-Spectrum β-Lactamases (ESBLs) which hydrolyze third-generation cephalosporins.
-   **Altered PBPs:** Reduced binding affinity.
-   **Reduced Permeability/Efflux:** Primarily in Gram-negative bacteria.

**Production:**
The initial cephalosporin nucleus (7-aminocephalosporanic acid, 7-ACA) is produced by fermentation using the fungus *Acremonium chrysogenum* (formerly *Cephalosporium acremonium*). Semi-synthetic modifications are then made to create different cephalosporin drugs.
