# 2.1.1.3 Carbapenems

Carbapenems are a class of [[2.1.1 β-Lactam Antibiotics|β-lactam antibiotics]] known for their exceptionally broad spectrum of activity and high potency, including against many multi-drug resistant (MDR) bacteria. They are often reserved for severe or complicated infections.

**Chemical Structure:**
Feature a β-lactam ring fused to a five-membered ring system that differs from penicillins (contains carbon instead of sulfur, and a double bond), conferring enhanced stability against many β-lactamases.

**Examples:**
-   **Imipenem:** First carbapenem developed. Very broad spectrum. Rapidly hydrolyzed by renal dehydropeptidase-I (DHP-I), so it's always co-administered with **cilastatin**, a DHP-I inhibitor, to increase its half-life and prevent nephrotoxicity.
-   **Meropenem:** Similar broad spectrum to imipenem but stable to DHP-I (does not require cilastatin). Lower seizure potential than imipenem.
-   **Ertapenem:** Slightly narrower spectrum than imipenem/meropenem (less activity against *Pseudomonas* and *Acinetobacter*), but has a longer half-life allowing once-daily dosing.
-   **Doripenem:** Broad spectrum similar to meropenem, potentially more stable in solution.

**Mechanism of Action:**
Like other β-lactams, carbapenems inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to and inactivating Penicillin-Binding Proteins (PBPs), preventing peptidoglycan cross-linking. Their broad spectrum is partly due to efficient penetration through porins (in Gram-negatives) and high affinity for multiple PBP types. (See [[2.3.1 Interference with Cell Wall Synthesis]])

**Spectrum of Activity:**
Very broad, covering most Gram-positive cocci (including MSSA but not MRSA), Gram-negative rods (including Enterobacteriaceae, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Acinetobacter* spp. - though ertapenem is weaker here), and anaerobes.

**Clinical Uses:**
Reserved for serious infections due to MDR organisms or polymicrobial infections:
-   Nosocomial pneumonia (HAP/VAP)
-   Complicated intra-abdominal infections
-   Complicated urinary tract infections (cUTIs)
-   Bacterial meningitis (meropenem)
-   Febrile neutropenia
-   Sepsis

**Resistance Mechanisms:**
Resistance is a growing concern.
-   **Carbapenemase Production:** Most significant mechanism. Bacteria produce enzymes (carbapenemases, e.g., KPC, NDM, VIM, IMP, OXA-48 type) that hydrolyze carbapenems. These genes are often plasmid-mediated and spread easily.
-   **Reduced Permeability:** Mutations leading to loss or modification of outer membrane porins (especially OprD for imipenem in *P. aeruginosa*).
-   **Efflux Pumps:** Overexpression can contribute to resistance.
-   **Altered PBPs:** Less common mechanism for carbapenem resistance.

**Production:**
The initial carbapenem structures (like thienamycin, the natural product precursor to imipenem) are often produced by fermentation using *Streptomyces* species. These natural products are then chemically modified to create clinically used carbapenems like imipenem and meropenem.
