# 2.1.2 Aminoglycosides

**Overview:**
Aminoglycosides are a class of potent, broad-spectrum, bactericidal antibiotics primarily used for serious infections caused by aerobic Gram-negative bacteria, and sometimes Gram-positive bacteria (often in combination therapy). They inhibit bacterial protein synthesis.

**Chemical Structure:**
Characterized by amino sugars linked via glycosidic bonds to an aminocyclitol ring (usually 2-deoxystreptamine). They are polycationic and highly polar, affecting their pharmacokinetic properties (poor oral absorption, limited CNS penetration, renal excretion).

**Examples:**
-   **Gentamicin:** Widely used, effective against *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, Enterobacteriaceae. Often used in combination with β-lactams for synergy against Gram-positives (e.g., endocarditis).
-   **Tobramycin:** Similar to gentamicin, slightly more active against *P. aeruginosa*. Available as inhaled formulation for cystic fibrosis.
-   **Amikacin:** Semi-synthetic derivative, broader spectrum due to resistance to many aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (AMEs). Often reserved for MDR infections.
-   **Streptomycin:** Historically important (first aminoglycoside discovered), used for tuberculosis (MDR-TB), plague, tularemia. Resistance is common.
-   **Neomycin:** Highly toxic systemically, used primarily topically (skin infections) or orally for bowel decontamination (poor absorption).
-   **Kanamycin, Netilmicin, Plazomicin.**

**Mechanism of Action:**
Aminoglycosides inhibit bacterial protein synthesis irreversibly. (See [[2.3.2 Inhibition of Protein Synthesis]])
1.  **Uptake:** Enter bacteria via an oxygen-dependent active transport process (hence ineffective against strict anaerobes). Initial binding to LPS/outer membrane facilitates uptake.
2.  **Binding to 30S Ribosome:** Bind primarily to the 16S rRNA within the 30S ribosomal subunit (specifically the A-site).
3.  **Interference with Protein Synthesis:** Binding causes:
    -   **Misreading of mRNA:** Incorporation of incorrect amino acids, leading to non-functional proteins.
    -   **Premature Termination:** Release of incomplete polypeptide chains.
    -   **Inhibition of Translocation:** Blocking the movement of the ribosome along the mRNA.
4.  **Bactericidal Effect:** Accumulation of non-functional proteins and potential membrane damage contribute to rapid bacterial killing.

**Clinical Uses:**
-   Serious Gram-negative infections (sepsis, pneumonia, complicated UTIs, intra-abdominal infections).
-   Synergistic use with cell wall active agents (β-lactams, vancomycin) for certain Gram-positive infections (e.g., *Staphylococcus*, *Enterococcus* endocarditis).
-   Tuberculosis (streptomycin, amikacin as second-line agents).
-   Plague, Tularemia (streptomycin, gentamicin).

**Side Effects (Toxicity):**
Use is limited by potential toxicities, requiring therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM).
-   **Nephrotoxicity:** Accumulation in renal proximal tubule cells, causing acute tubular necrosis. Usually reversible upon discontinuation. Risk increases with dose, duration, pre-existing renal impairment, concurrent nephrotoxins.
-   **Ototoxicity:** Damage to cochlear (hearing loss - often irreversible) and/or vestibular (balance problems) hair cells. Risk factors similar to nephrotoxicity.
-   **Neuromuscular Blockade:** Rare, but can occur at high doses or with rapid IV infusion, especially in patients with myasthenia gravis or receiving neuromuscular blockers. Can cause respiratory paralysis.

**Resistance Mechanisms:**
-   **Enzymatic Modification (AMEs):** Most common mechanism. Bacteria produce enzymes (acetyltransferases, phosphotransferases, nucleotidyltransferases) that modify the aminoglycoside, preventing ribosomal binding. Genes often on plasmids/transposons. (Amikacin is resistant to many AMEs).
-   **Reduced Uptake/Permeability:** Alterations in transport systems or outer membrane.
-   **Target Site Modification:** Mutations in 16S rRNA reducing drug binding (less common).

**Production:**
Produced naturally by fermentation, primarily using various species of actinomycetes, especially *Streptomyces* (e.g., streptomycin from *S. griseus*) and *Micromonospora* (e.g., gentamicin from *M. purpurea*).
