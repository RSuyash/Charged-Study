# 2.1.4 Macrolides

**Overview:**
Macrolides are a class of primarily bacteriostatic antibiotics characterized by a large macrocyclic lactone ring. They are important alternatives for patients allergic to β-lactams and are effective against many Gram-positive bacteria and atypical pathogens.

**Chemical Structure:**
Contain a large lactone ring (typically 14-, 15-, or 16-membered) to which one or more deoxy sugars (like desosamine and cladinose) are attached via glycosidic bonds.

![Erythromycin Structure](<../../../V2/Pharmaceutical Biotechnology/Unit II 1a63a995df87804baa28f5eb2cc772fe/image%2010.png>)
*Fig: Molecular structure of erythromycin (a 14-membered macrolide). Lactone ring in red, deoxy sugars in blue.*

**Examples:**
-   **Erythromycin:** The prototype macrolide (14-membered ring). Effective against Gram-positives (*Streptococcus*, some *Staphylococcus*), atypical bacteria (*Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydia*, *Legionella*), and some Gram-negatives (*Bordetella pertussis*, *Campylobacter*). Used for respiratory infections, skin infections, diphtheria, pertussis. Can cause significant GI upset and has drug interactions (CYP3A4 inhibitor).
-   **Clarithromycin:** Semi-synthetic derivative of erythromycin (14-membered ring). Improved acid stability, better oral absorption, broader spectrum (includes *H. influenzae*, *H. pylori*), longer half-life than erythromycin. Fewer GI side effects. Also inhibits CYP3A4.
-   **Azithromycin (Zithromax):** Semi-synthetic derivative (15-membered ring - azalide). Excellent tissue penetration and very long half-life (allowing once-daily dosing and shorter courses). Broader Gram-negative activity (including *H. influenzae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*) than erythromycin. Less GI upset and fewer drug interactions (weak CYP3A4 inhibitor). Widely used for respiratory infections (CAP), STIs (*Chlamydia*).
-   *Others:* Roxithromycin, Dirithromycin, Telithromycin (a ketolide, derivative with broader activity but associated with liver toxicity).

**Mechanism of Action:**
Macrolides inhibit bacterial protein synthesis by binding reversibly to the 50S ribosomal subunit. (See [[2.3.2 Inhibition of Protein Synthesis]])
1.  **Binding to 50S Ribosome:** Bind to the 23S rRNA component within the 50S subunit, near the peptidyl transferase center and the polypeptide exit tunnel.
2.  **Inhibition of Translocation/Transpeptidation:** Block the exit of the growing peptide chain and/or interfere with the translocation step (movement of peptidyl-tRNA from A-site to P-site), thus inhibiting peptide bond formation and elongation.
3.  **Bacteriostatic Effect:** Inhibition of protein synthesis stops bacterial growth. At high concentrations, they can be bactericidal against some highly susceptible organisms.

**Spectrum of Activity:**
-   Gram-positive cocci (*Streptococcus pyogenes*, *S. pneumoniae* - resistance increasing).
-   Atypical pathogens (*Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, *Chlamydia pneumoniae*, *Legionella pneumophila*).
-   Some Gram-negatives (*Bordetella pertussis*, *Campylobacter jejuni*, *H. influenzae* - especially azithromycin/clarithromycin).
-   *Helicobacter pylori* (clarithromycin used in combination therapy).

**Clinical Uses:**
-   Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), especially atypical pneumonia.
-   Upper respiratory tract infections (pharyngitis, sinusitis - especially in penicillin-allergic patients).
-   Pertussis (whooping cough).
-   Diphtheria (adjunct to antitoxin).
-   Chlamydial infections.
-   *H. pylori* eradication (clarithromycin).
-   Certain skin and soft tissue infections.

**Side Effects:**
-   **Gastrointestinal Disturbances:** Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain (most common, especially with erythromycin due to motilin receptor agonism).
-   **QT Prolongation:** Can prolong the QT interval on ECG, increasing risk of potentially fatal cardiac arrhythmias (Torsades de Pointes). Risk higher with erythromycin, less with azithromycin. Caution with other QT-prolonging drugs.
-   **Hepatotoxicity:** Cholestatic hepatitis (rare, primarily associated with erythromycin estolate).
-   **Drug Interactions:** Erythromycin and clarithromycin are potent inhibitors of the cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP3A4, increasing levels of co-administered drugs metabolized by this enzyme (e.g., statins, warfarin, cyclosporine). Azithromycin is a much weaker inhibitor.

**Resistance Mechanisms:**
Resistance is common and increasing.
-   **Target Site Modification (erm genes):** Methylation of the 23S rRNA binding site by Erm methyltransferases prevents macrolide binding. Most common mechanism, often confers cross-resistance to macrolides, lincosamides, and streptogramin B (MLS<sub>B</sub> resistance). Often plasmid-mediated.
-   **Efflux Pumps (mef genes):** Active transport of macrolides out of the bacterial cell (e.g., Mef pumps). Confers resistance primarily to 14- and 15-membered macrolides.
-   **Enzymatic Inactivation:** Less common mechanism (e.g., esterases).

**Production:**
Erythromycin is produced by fermentation using the bacterium *Saccharopolyspora erythraea* (formerly *Streptomyces erythreus*). Clarithromycin and azithromycin are semi-synthetic derivatives made by chemical modification of erythromycin.
