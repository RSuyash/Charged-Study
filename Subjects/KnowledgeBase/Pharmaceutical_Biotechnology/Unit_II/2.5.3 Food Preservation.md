# 2.5.3 Food Preservation Applications

Certain antimicrobial agents, particularly those produced naturally by microorganisms (bacteriocins), are used in the food industry as preservatives to inhibit the growth of spoilage and pathogenic bacteria, thereby extending shelf life and enhancing food safety.

**Applications & Examples:**

-   **Bacteriocins:** These are ribosomally synthesized antimicrobial peptides produced by bacteria that inhibit the growth of closely related bacterial species.
    -   **Nisin:** Produced by *Lactococcus lactis* subsp. *lactis*. It is effective primarily against Gram-positive bacteria, including spoilage organisms and pathogens like *Listeria monocytogenes*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, and spore-forming bacteria (*Clostridium*, *Bacillus*).
        -   *Mechanism:* Nisin binds to Lipid II (a peptidoglycan precursor) and forms pores in the bacterial cell membrane, leading to leakage of cellular contents and dissipation of membrane potential.
        -   *Uses:* Approved food additive (E234) used in processed cheese, dairy products, canned foods, meat products, and beverages to prevent spoilage and inhibit pathogens. It is generally recognized as safe (GRAS).
    -   **Pediocin:** Produced by *Pediococcus* species. Effective against *Listeria monocytogenes*. Used in meat products.
-   **Other Antimicrobials:**
    -   While direct addition of clinical antibiotics to food for preservation is generally prohibited due to concerns about [[2.4 Microbial Resistance to Antibiotics and Antimicrobial Agents|antimicrobial resistance]] and potential allergic reactions, some naturally occurring antimicrobial compounds derived from plants ([[1.2.4 Other Classes of Secondary Metabolites|Essential oils]], organic acids) are used.
    -   Antibiotics might be used indirectly, e.g., treating animal diseases (see [[2.5.2 Veterinary Medicine]]), with regulations ensuring residues are below safe limits in food products.

**Regulatory Considerations:**
-   The use of antimicrobial agents as food preservatives is strictly regulated by food safety authorities (e.g., FDA in the US, EFSA in the EU).
-   Only approved substances with demonstrated safety and efficacy can be used, often with specified maximum usage levels and labeling requirements.
-   Focus is generally on naturally derived antimicrobials like bacteriocins or organic acids rather than clinical antibiotics.
