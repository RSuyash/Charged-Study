# 2.6.2 Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC)

**Definition:**
The Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) is the **lowest concentration** of an antimicrobial agent (e.g., antibiotic) that prevents the **visible growth** of a specific microorganism *in vitro* after a defined incubation period (typically 18-24 hours). It is a standard laboratory measure of the potency or effectiveness of an antimicrobial against a particular microbe.

![MIC Determination Example](<../../../V2/Pharmaceutical Biotechnology/Unit II 1a63a995df87804baa28f5eb2cc772fe/image%2036.png>)
*Fig: Example showing MIC determination via broth microdilution.*

**Units:**
MIC values are typically reported in mass per volume, such as micrograms per milliliter (µg/mL) or milligrams per liter (mg/L).

**Methods for Determining MIC:**
MIC is primarily determined using quantitative dilution methods:

1.  **Broth Dilution Method:** (See [[2.6.1 Principle and Methods of Microbial Assay]])
    -   *Principle:* Serial dilutions of the antimicrobial are prepared in broth, inoculated with a standardized bacterial suspension, and incubated.
    -   *Determination:* The MIC is the lowest concentration tube/well showing no visible turbidity (growth).
    -   *Formats:* Macrodilution (tubes) or Microdilution (96-well plates - common in clinical labs and automation).
    -   *MBC:* Can be extended to determine Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC) by subculturing from clear wells onto antibiotic-free agar. MBC is the lowest concentration killing ≥99.9% of the initial inoculum.

    ![Broth Microdilution Schematic](<../../../V2/Pharmaceutical Biotechnology/Unit II 1a63a995df87804baa28f5eb2cc772fe/image%2037.png>)
    *Fig: Schematic of broth microdilution process.*

2.  **Agar Dilution Method:** (See [[2.6.1 Principle and Methods of Microbial Assay]])
    -   *Principle:* Serial dilutions of the antimicrobial are incorporated into agar plates.
    -   *Determination:* Standardized inocula are spotted onto the plates. The MIC is the lowest concentration plate showing no visible growth (or significant reduction). Allows testing multiple strains simultaneously.

    ![Agar Dilution with NPs](<../../../V2/Pharmaceutical Biotechnology/Unit II 1a63a995df87804baa28f5eb2cc772fe/image%2038.png>)
    *Fig: Agar dilution method example.*

3.  **Gradient Strip Method (E-test):**
    -   *Principle:* A plastic strip impregnated with a predefined, continuous concentration gradient of an antibiotic is placed on an inoculated agar plate. The drug diffuses into the agar.
    -   *Determination:* After incubation, an elliptical zone of inhibition forms. The MIC is read directly from a scale printed on the strip at the point where the edge of the inhibition zone intersects the strip.
    -   *Advantages:* Provides a direct MIC reading, relatively easy to perform and interpret.

    ![E-test Gradient Strip](<../../../V2/Pharmaceutical Biotechnology/Unit II 1a63a995df87804baa28f5eb2cc772fe/image%2039.png>)
    *Fig: E-test gradient strip method.*

4.  **Automated Systems:**
    -   *Principle:* Commercial systems (e.g., VITEK, MicroScan, Phoenix) automate broth microdilution testing and interpretation using instrumented reading of growth (e.g., turbidity, fluorescence).
    -   *Advantages:* Rapid results, high throughput, standardization, integrated expert systems for interpretation.

    ![Automated System Example](<../../../V2/Pharmaceutical Biotechnology/Unit II 1a63a995df87804baa28f5eb2cc772fe/image%2040.png>)
    *Fig: Example of an automated susceptibility testing system.*

**Significance and Applications:**

-   **Clinical Guidance:** MIC values help clinicians select appropriate antibiotics and dosages. Lower MIC suggests higher potency. Results are interpreted using clinical breakpoints.
-   **Resistance Monitoring:** Tracking MIC distributions over time helps monitor trends in bacterial susceptibility and detect emerging resistance (MIC creep). Essential for public health surveillance.
-   **Drug Discovery & Development:** Used to evaluate the *in vitro* potency of new antimicrobial candidates and compare them to existing drugs.
-   **PK/PD Studies:** MIC is a key parameter in pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) modeling, which relates drug exposure (concentration over time) to antimicrobial effect (e.g., Time > MIC, Peak/MIC, AUC/MIC ratios) to optimize dosing regimens.

**Interpretation (Clinical Breakpoints):**
-   **Breakpoints:** MIC values alone don't define clinical success. Regulatory bodies (CLSI, EUCAST) establish clinical breakpoints for specific drug-bug combinations. These are MIC thresholds used to categorize an isolate as:
    -   **Susceptible (S):** High likelihood of therapeutic success with standard dosing.
    -   **Intermediate (I) / Susceptible - Increased Exposure (I/S-IE):** Therapeutic success possible with increased exposure (higher dose, modified frequency) or if drug concentrates at infection site.
    -   **Resistant (R):** High likelihood of therapeutic failure even with increased exposure.
-   **Factors:** Breakpoints consider MIC distributions, PK/PD data, mechanisms of resistance, and clinical outcome data.

MIC is a fundamental quantitative measure in antimicrobial susceptibility testing.
