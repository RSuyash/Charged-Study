# 2.2.2 Pharmacokinetic Properties (ADME)

**Pharmacokinetics (PK)** describes the movement of drugs within the body over time – essentially "what the body does to the drug." Understanding the PK properties of an antimicrobial is crucial for determining appropriate dosing regimens (dose, frequency, duration) to ensure effective drug concentrations reach the site of infection while minimizing toxicity. PK is often summarized by the acronym **ADME**: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion.

![ADME Schematic](<../../../V2/Pharmaceutical Biotechnology/Unit II 1a63a995df87804baa28f5eb2cc772fe/image%2020.png>)
*Fig: Schematic diagram showing the four pharmacokinetic processes: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion (ADME) including their pharmacokinetic parameters.*

**1. Absorption:**
-   **Definition:** The process by which the drug moves from the site of administration into the systemic circulation (bloodstream).
-   **Factors Influencing Absorption:**
    -   *Route of Administration:*
        -   **Intravenous (IV):** Bypasses absorption, 100% bioavailability immediately.
        -   **Intramuscular (IM) / Subcutaneous (SC):** Absorption depends on blood flow at the injection site.
        -   **Oral (PO):** Most common route, but absorption can be variable. Affected by:
            -   Drug formulation (tablet, capsule, liquid).
            -   Drug physicochemical properties (solubility, lipophilicity, pKa).
            -   Gastric pH, gastric emptying time.
            -   Presence of food (can increase, decrease, or not affect absorption depending on the drug).
            -   First-pass metabolism (metabolism in the gut wall or liver before reaching systemic circulation).
    -   *Bioavailability (F):* The fraction (%) of the administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged. IV bioavailability is 100%; oral bioavailability varies widely.

**2. Distribution:**
-   **Definition:** The reversible transfer of a drug from the bloodstream to various tissues and fluids of the body.
-   **Factors Influencing Distribution:**
    -   *Volume of Distribution (Vd):* An apparent volume representing the extent to which a drug distributes into body tissues compared to plasma. High Vd suggests extensive tissue distribution (drug leaves plasma); Low Vd suggests drug remains primarily in the bloodstream.
    -   *Tissue Permeability:* Ability of the drug to cross biological membranes (e.g., blood-brain barrier, placental barrier). Depends on lipophilicity, size, charge, and active transport.
    -   *Plasma Protein Binding:* Many drugs bind reversibly to plasma proteins (mainly albumin). Only the unbound (free) fraction is pharmacologically active and able to distribute into tissues and be eliminated. High protein binding can:
        -   Limit distribution.
        -   Act as a reservoir for the drug.
        -   Lead to drug interactions if one drug displaces another from binding sites (e.g., warfarin displacement).
    -   *Blood Flow:* Tissues with higher blood flow (brain, liver, kidneys) receive the drug more rapidly than tissues with lower blood flow (fat, bone).

**3. Metabolism (Biotransformation):**
-   **Definition:** The biochemical modification of drugs by the body, primarily in the liver, to make them more water-soluble and easier to excrete.
-   **Phases of Metabolism:**
    -   *Phase I Reactions:* Introduce or expose functional groups (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis). Often mediated by Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes. Can result in active, inactive, or sometimes toxic metabolites.
    -   *Phase II Reactions:* Conjugation reactions where an endogenous molecule (e.g., glucuronic acid, sulfate, glutathione) is attached to the drug or its Phase I metabolite, increasing water solubility for excretion.
-   **Factors Influencing Metabolism:**
    -   *Liver Function:* Impaired liver function can decrease drug metabolism, leading to accumulation and potential toxicity.
    -   *Genetic Polymorphisms:* Variations in genes encoding metabolic enzymes (e.g., CYP enzymes) can cause significant inter-individual differences in drug metabolism rates (poor vs. extensive vs. ultra-rapid metabolizers).
    -   *Drug Interactions:* Some drugs can **induce** (speed up) or **inhibit** (slow down) metabolic enzymes (especially CYP450s), affecting the metabolism and levels of other co-administered drugs.
        -   *Inducers (e.g., rifampicin):* Decrease levels/efficacy of other drugs.
        -   *Inhibitors (e.g., erythromycin, clarithromycin, azole antifungals):* Increase levels/toxicity of other drugs.

**4. Excretion (Elimination):**
-   **Definition:** The irreversible removal of the drug and its metabolites from the body.
-   **Major Routes of Excretion:**
    -   *Renal Excretion (Kidneys):* Most common route. Involves glomerular filtration, active tubular secretion, and passive tubular reabsorption. Drug must be sufficiently water-soluble.
    -   *Hepatic/Biliary Excretion (Liver/Bile):* Drug/metabolites secreted into bile, then eliminated in feces. May involve enterohepatic recirculation (reabsorption from gut).
    -   *Other Routes:* Lungs (volatile drugs), sweat, saliva, breast milk (minor routes).
-   **Factors Influencing Excretion:**
    -   *Renal Function:* Impaired kidney function (measured by creatinine clearance or GFR) significantly reduces excretion of renally cleared drugs, requiring dose adjustments to prevent accumulation and toxicity (e.g., aminoglycosides, vancomycin, many β-lactams).
    -   *Liver Function:* Affects biliary excretion.
    -   *Urine pH:* Can influence reabsorption of weak acids/bases.
-   **Key PK Parameters:**
    -   *Clearance (CL):* Volume of plasma cleared of drug per unit time. Represents efficiency of elimination.
    -   *Half-life (t½):* Time required for the plasma concentration of a drug to decrease by 50%. Determines dosing frequency and time to reach steady state.

Understanding ADME properties is essential for optimizing antimicrobial therapy.
