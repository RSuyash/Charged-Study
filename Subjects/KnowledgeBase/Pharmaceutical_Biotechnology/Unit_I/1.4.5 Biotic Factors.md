# 1.4.5 Biotic Factors

Biotic factors refer to the influences of living organisms on the production of secondary metabolites in plants. These interactions often involve defense responses or symbiotic relationships.

1.  **Influence of Pathogens and Herbivores:**
    *   **Induction of Defense:** Attack by pathogens (fungi, bacteria, viruses) or feeding by herbivores (insects, mammals) are major triggers for the induction of defensive secondary metabolites.
    *   **Mechanism:** Plants recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or herbivore-associated molecular patterns (HAMPs), activating signaling pathways (often involving jasmonic acid, salicylic acid, ethylene) that lead to the synthesis of phytoalexins (antimicrobial compounds), feeding deterrents (alkaloids, tannins, saponins), toxins, or volatile compounds that attract natural enemies of herbivores (indirect defense).
    *   *Example:* Insect feeding can increase nicotine (alkaloid) levels in tobacco plants. Fungal infection can induce resveratrol (stilbene) production in grapes.

2.  **Role of Symbiotic Relationships:**
    *   **Mycorrhizae:** Symbiotic association between fungi and plant roots. Mycorrhizal fungi enhance nutrient (especially phosphorus) and water uptake by the plant, improving overall plant health and stress tolerance. This improved status can indirectly influence secondary metabolite profiles, sometimes increasing the production of certain beneficial compounds (e.g., phenolics, terpenoids). The fungi themselves might also produce signaling molecules affecting plant metabolism.
    *   **Rhizobia:** Nitrogen-fixing bacteria forming nodules on legume roots. By providing fixed nitrogen, rhizobia improve plant growth and nitrogen metabolism, which can indirectly affect the production of nitrogen-containing secondary metabolites (like alkaloids) or influence the C/N balance affecting other pathways.
    - **Endophytes:** Fungi or bacteria living within plant tissues without causing disease. Endophytes can produce their own secondary metabolites, some of which may be beneficial to the host plant (e.g., providing defense) or may be novel compounds of pharmaceutical interest (e.g., paclitaxel originally found in yew trees is also produced by endophytic fungi). Endophytes can also influence the host plant's secondary metabolism.

Understanding these biotic interactions is important for agriculture (plant resistance) and for potentially harnessing these interactions to enhance metabolite production in cultivated plants or culture systems.
