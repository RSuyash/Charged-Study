# Bioaugmentation

Bioaugmentation is an **[[In-situ Bioremediation|in-situ]]** (or sometimes [[Ex-situ Bioremediation|ex-situ]]) [[Bioremediation]] technology involving the addition of specific, pre-grown [[Microorganisms]] (augmentation cultures) to a contaminated environment to enhance the [[Biodegradation]] of target pollutants. It is applied when the indigenous microbial community lacks the necessary numbers or metabolic capabilities for effective degradation.

## Process Design and Operation

1.  **Site Characterization & Microbial Assessment:** Understand contamination, site conditions (hydrogeology, chemistry), and assess indigenous microbial community's degradation potential (via molecular analysis, microcosm studies). Determine if bioaugmentation is needed.
2.  **Selection/Development of Augmentation Cultures:** Choose or develop microbial cultures that are:
    *   *Effective Degraders:* Efficiently degrade target pollutants.
    *   *Adapted:* Can survive/function under site conditions (temp, pH, salinity, etc.).
    *   *Safe:* Non-pathogenic, environmentally compatible.
    *   *Competitive/Persistent (Ideally):* Can compete with indigenous microbes and persist long enough.
    *   *Types:* Enriched indigenous microbes, exogenous microbes (from other sites or commercial products), GMOs (less common due to regulations).
3.  **Preparation/Application of Cultures:** Grow augmentation cultures, prepare for application (cell suspension or immobilized on carriers like biochar, compost, alginate beads for better delivery/retention). Apply via injection wells (groundwater), surface application (soil), or mixing (ex-situ).
4.  **Monitoring/Performance Evaluation:** Track pollutant reduction, survival/activity of augmented microbes, biodegradation rates, and environmental parameters.

## Operational Considerations

-   **Delivery/Dispersal:** Ensuring effective distribution throughout the contaminated zone is critical.
-   **Survival/Persistence:** Augmented microbes must survive environmental stresses and competition from indigenous populations.
-   **Competition:** Introduced microbes must compete effectively for resources.
-   **Environmental Compatibility/Safety:** Must not disrupt native ecosystem or pose risks.
-   **Regulatory Approval:** May be required, especially for exogenous or GMO strains.

## Applications

-   Remediation of sites with recalcitrant pollutants (chlorinated solvents, pesticides, complex hydrocarbons like PAHs) poorly degraded by indigenous microbes.
-   Accelerating bioremediation at slow or stalled sites.
-   Targeting specific pollutants with specialized degraders.
-   Often combined with other methods like [[Biostimulation]], [[Bioventing]], [[Biosparging]].

## Advantages

-   *In-situ* treatment possible.
-   Can enhance degradation of recalcitrant compounds.
-   Can accelerate cleanup rates.
-   Can be tailored to specific pollutants.

## Limitations

-   Survival/persistence of introduced microbes can be challenging.
-   Competition with indigenous microbes may limit effectiveness.
-   Environmental compatibility/safety/regulatory concerns.
-   Requires thorough site assessment and careful selection/application of microbes.
-   May not work if environmental conditions are limiting [[2.1.3 Factors Affecting Biodegradation|biodegradation]].

Bioaugmentation can be a powerful tool when indigenous microbial activity is insufficient, but requires careful planning and execution.
