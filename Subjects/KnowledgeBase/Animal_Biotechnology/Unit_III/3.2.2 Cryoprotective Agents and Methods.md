# 3.2.2 Cryoprotective Agents and Methods

[[3.2.1 Principles of Cryopreservation|Cryoprotective agents (CPAs)]] are essential for minimizing cell damage during [[3.2 Cryopreservation]]. They are combined with specific freezing methods.

## Cryoprotective Agents (CPAs)

![Fig. List of Cryoprotective Agents](<../../../V2/Animal Biotechnology/AB-Unit-III/Unit III 1a53a995df878024b76bc10d633533df/image 10.png>)
*Fig. List of Cryoprotective Agents*

1.  **Permeating CPAs (Intracellular):** Enter cells, protect inside and out.
    *   **[[DMSO]] (Dimethyl Sulfoxide):** Most widely used. Effective, broad applicability. Used at 5-10% (v/v). Can be toxic at higher concentrations/prolonged exposure, may induce differentiation (stem cells). Requires removal after thawing.
    *   **[[Glycerol]]:** Common alternative. Less toxic than DMSO for some cells. Used at 5-20% (v/v). May be less effective than DMSO for some cells, increases viscosity. Requires removal after thawing.
    *   **Ethylene Glycol (EG):** Rapidly permeating. Primarily used for oocyte/embryo cryopreservation.
    *   **Propylene Glycol:** Another permeating CPA.

2.  **Non-Permeating CPAs (Extracellular):** Protect primarily from outside.
    *   **[[Sucrose]]:** Disaccharide sugar. Non-toxic, inexpensive. Reduces extracellular solution effects, stabilizes membranes (water replacement). Used at 0.1-0.3 M. Less effective alone than permeating CPAs.
    *   **[[Trehalose]]:** Non-reducing disaccharide. Excellent cryoprotection, non-toxic, effective membrane/protein stabilizer (water replacement). Found in anhydrobiotic organisms. Used at 0.1-0.3 M. More expensive than sucrose, requires methods (electroporation, liposomes) for intracellular delivery if needed.
    *   **Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP):** Synthetic polymer. Reduces solution effects, increases viscosity. Used in some protocols (embryos, tissues).

**Combinations:** Often used (e.g., DMSO + Sucrose) for optimal protection.

## Cryopreservation Methods

![Fig. Methods of Cryopreservation](<../../../V2/Animal Biotechnology/AB-Unit-III/Unit III 1a53a995df878024b76bc10d633533df/image 11.png>)
*Fig. Methods of Cryopreservation*

1.  **Slow Freezing (Controlled-Rate Freezing):**
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  Prepare cryopreservation medium (base medium + serum/protein + CPA(s)).
        2.  Suspend cells in cryo-medium.
        3.  Dispense into [[Cryovials]].
        4.  Cool slowly (-1°C to -3°C/min) using a controlled-rate freezer down to -40°C or -80°C.
        5.  Transfer to long-term storage ([[LN2]]).
        ![Fig. Slow Freezing](<../../../V2/Animal Biotechnology/AB-Unit-III/Unit III 1a53a995df878024b76bc10d633533df/image 12.png>)
        *Fig. Slow Freezing*
    *   **Advantages:** Widely applicable, robust, effective for many cell types, well-established.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Requires controlled-rate freezer, slower process than vitrification.

2.  **Vitrification (Rapid Freezing to Glassy State):**
    *   **Principle:** Ultra-rapid freezing avoids ice crystal formation by solidifying into a glass-like state. Requires very high CPA concentrations and extremely rapid cooling rates.
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  Prepare highly concentrated vitrification solution (high levels of permeating/non-permeating CPAs).
        2.  Equilibrate cells briefly in vitrification solution.
        3.  Rapidly plunge samples (small volumes, thin carriers like straws) directly into [[LN2]] (-196°C). Cooling rates: thousands of °C/min.
        4.  Store in LN2.
        5.  Thaw very rapidly (e.g., plunge into 37°C water bath) to avoid devitrification (ice recrystallization).
        ![Fig. A. Vitrification and B. Encapsulation-dehydration.](<../../../V2/Animal Biotechnology/AB-Unit-III/Unit III 1a53a995df878024b76bc10d633533df/image 13.png>)
        *Fig. A. Vitrification and B. Encapsulation-dehydration.*
    *   **Advantages:** Theoretically eliminates ice damage, potentially very high viability, may preserve ultrastructure better.
    *   **Limitations:** High CPA concentrations can be toxic/cause osmotic stress, technically complex (requires optimization of CPA, cooling/warming rates), limited to small sample volumes, risk of devitrification during warming if not rapid enough.
