# Solvent Precipitation

Solvent precipitation is a [[2.1.1 Precipitation Methods|precipitation method]] used in [[2.1 Removal and Recovery of Cell Mass|cell mass removal]] and protein purification. It involves adding water-miscible organic solvents to reduce the dielectric constant of the medium, decreasing protein solubility and promoting aggregation.

## Principle

Organic solvents disrupt the hydration layer around proteins, increasing hydrophobic interactions and causing precipitation.

## Technique

Cold organic solvent (e.g., ethanol, acetone, chilled to -20°C or lower) is slowly added to the fermentation broth, typically under stirring and at low temperatures to minimize protein denaturation. The mixture is then allowed to settle or centrifuged to separate the precipitated cell mass or proteins.

## Applications

-   Cell mass removal.
-   Protein precipitation.
-   Ethanol precipitation is commonly used for DNA/RNA precipitation in molecular biology.

## Advantages

-   Effective for precipitating proteins and other biomolecules.
-   Can be used at low temperatures to minimize protein denaturation.

## Limitations

-   Non-selective: Precipitates a mixture of cellular components.
-   Organic solvents can be flammable and require explosion-proof equipment.
-   May denature some proteins.
-   Requires solvent removal and recovery steps.
-   Solvent disposal can be an issue.

Solvent precipitation is a useful technique, but requires careful control to avoid product degradation and manage solvent hazards.
