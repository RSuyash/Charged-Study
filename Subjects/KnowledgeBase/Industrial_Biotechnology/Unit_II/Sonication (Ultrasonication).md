# Sonication (Ultrasonication)

Sonication, or ultrasonication, is a [[2.2.1 Physical Methods of Cell Disruption|physical method]] used for [[2.2 Cell Disruption|cell disruption]] and other applications. It uses high-frequency sound waves to generate cavitation and mechanical shear forces in a liquid.

## Principle

Ultrasound waves create cavitation bubbles (formation, growth, and violent collapse of microscopic bubbles) in the liquid. The collapse generates localized high pressure and shear forces that disrupt cell membranes and cell walls, leading to lysis.

## Technique

A sonicator probe or bath is immersed in the cell suspension. Electrical energy is converted to mechanical vibrations, generating ultrasound waves.

-   **Probe Sonicators (Direct Immersion):** More efficient for small volumes/batch processing. Can generate significant heat.
-   **Bath Sonicators (Indirect):** Sample in a tube immersed in a sonicated water bath. Less efficient, generates less heat, allows simultaneous processing of multiple samples.
-   **Continuous Flow Sonicators:** For continuous processing of large volumes.

## Operational Parameters and Control

-   **Sonication Intensity (Amplitude):** Higher amplitude increases cavitation/disruption, but also heat.
-   **Sonication Time/Duration:** Longer times increase disruption, but also heat/degradation. Pulsed sonication (on/off cycles) reduces heat.
-   **Duty Cycle (Pulsed):** Percentage of time sonicator is on during a cycle.
-   **Temperature Control:** Cooling systems (ice bath, circulating water) are essential to prevent protein denaturation.
-   **Sample Volume/Concentration:** Influence sonication efficiency.

## Applications

-   Effective for disrupting bacteria, yeast, mammalian cells, and tissues at lab/pilot scales.
-   Releasing intracellular enzymes, proteins, DNA, biomolecules.

## Advantages

-   Effective cell disruption.
-   Relatively fast.
-   Versatile for different cell types.
-   Widely available laboratory sonicators.

## Limitations

-   Generates significant heat (requires cooling).
-   Not easily scalable for large-scale industrial processing.
-   Probe sonicators can cause sample contamination (titanium particle release).
-   Noise generation.
