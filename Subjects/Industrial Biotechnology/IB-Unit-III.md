## 3.1 Enzyme Production

Enzymes are biological catalysts that play a crucial role in various industrial sectors, including food, textiles, detergents, pharmaceuticals, and biofuels. Microbial fermentation is a major route for the large-scale production of industrial enzymes.

### 3.1.1 Protease

Proteases, also known as peptidases or proteinases, are enzymes that catalyze the hydrolysis of peptide bonds in proteins, breaking them down into smaller peptides or amino acids. Proteases are one of the most important classes of industrial enzymes, accounting for a significant share of the global enzyme market.

**3.1.1.1 Microorganisms Involved in Protease Production**

A wide variety of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, and actinomycetes, are capable of producing proteases. Industrially important protease-producing microorganisms include:

- **Bacteria:**
    - ***Bacillus subtilis* and other *Bacillus* species:** *Bacillus* species are among the most important industrial producers of bacterial proteases. They are Gram-positive, spore-forming bacteria, known for their ability to secrete large quantities of extracellular proteases. Examples include *Bacillus subtilis*, *Bacillus licheniformis*, *Bacillus amyloliquefaciens*. These bacteria produce alkaline proteases, neutral proteases, and subtilisins, which are widely used in detergents, food processing, and textile industries.
    - ***Pseudomonas* species:** Some *Pseudomonas* species are also capable of producing proteases, although they are less commonly used for large-scale industrial production compared to *Bacillus*.
- **Fungi:**
    - ***Aspergillus niger* and other *Aspergillus* species:** *Aspergillus* fungi are important producers of fungal proteases, particularly acid proteases and metalloproteases. *Aspergillus niger* is widely used in industrial enzyme production due to its GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) status and high secretion capacity.
    - ***Trichoderma reesei*:** While primarily known for cellulase production, *Trichoderma reesei* also produces proteases as part of its cellulolytic enzyme complex.
- **Actinomycetes:**
    - ***Streptomyces griseus* and other *Streptomyces* species:** *Streptomyces* are filamentous bacteria known for producing a wide range of extracellular enzymes, including proteases. Some *Streptomyces* proteases have applications in leather processing and pharmaceuticals.

The choice of microorganism depends on the desired protease characteristics (pH optimum, temperature optimum, substrate specificity, protease type - serine, metallo, aspartic, cysteine), production cost, and downstream processing considerations.

**3.1.1.2 Fermentation Process for Protease Production**

Protease production through fermentation is typically carried out using submerged fermentation (SmF) or solid-state fermentation (SSF), depending on the microorganism and the specific protease being produced.

![Fig. Fermentation Process for Enzyme Production](Unit III 1a63a995df878010bfefd5d2faf7d80a/image.png)

Fig. Fermentation Process for Enzyme Production

**Submerged Fermentation (SmF) for Protease Production:**

- **Raw Materials/Substrates:**
    - **Carbon Source:** Carbohydrates (glucose, starch, molasses), oils (vegetable oils, animal fats), glycerol. The choice of carbon source can influence protease production levels and enzyme characteristics.
    - **Nitrogen Source:** Organic nitrogen sources are often preferred for protease production as they can also act as inducers. Examples include:
        - **Proteinaceous Materials:** Soybean meal, casein, corn steep liquor, yeast extract, peptone, meat extract, gelatin. These complex nitrogen sources provide amino acids and peptides that induce protease production and serve as building blocks for enzyme synthesis.
        - **Inorganic Nitrogen Sources:** Ammonium salts (ammonium sulfate, ammonium nitrate) can be used as supplementary nitrogen sources.
    - **Minerals:** Essential minerals (phosphorus, magnesium, calcium, iron, zinc, manganese) are required for microbial growth and enzyme synthesis.
    - **Growth Factors:** Some microorganisms may require specific growth factors (vitamins, amino acids) for optimal protease production.
- **Fermentation Conditions:**
    - **Fermentation Type:** Typically **batch fermentation** or **fed-batch fermentation**.
        - **Batch Fermentation:** All nutrients are added at the beginning of the fermentation. Simple to operate but substrate depletion and byproduct accumulation can limit protease production.
        - **Fed-Batch Fermentation:** Nutrients (carbon source, nitrogen source) are fed intermittently or continuously during fermentation to maintain optimal substrate concentrations, extend the production phase, and achieve higher protease yields. Fed-batch is commonly used for industrial protease production.
    - **Aeration:** **Aerobic fermentation**. Protease production by most industrial microorganisms is aerobic, requiring sufficient dissolved oxygen for microbial growth and enzyme synthesis. Aeration is provided through sparging air or oxygen into the fermenter and agitation to enhance oxygen transfer.
    - **Temperature:** Temperature is controlled within the optimal range for the specific microorganism, typically in the mesophilic range (25-37°C) for *Bacillus* and *Pseudomonas*, and slightly lower (25-30°C) for *Aspergillus* and *Trichoderma*.
    - **pH:** pH is controlled within the optimal range for microbial growth and protease production, typically near neutral to slightly alkaline (pH 7.0-8.5) for bacterial alkaline proteases and slightly acidic to neutral (pH 5.0-7.0) for fungal acid proteases. pH is controlled using buffers or by automatic addition of acid or base.
    - **Agitation:** Agitation is used to ensure proper mixing of nutrients, oxygen distribution, and temperature uniformity within the fermenter. Agitation intensity is optimized to balance mixing and aeration with shear sensitivity of microorganisms.
    - **Inoculum Development:** A well-prepared and active inoculum is crucial for consistent and efficient fermentation. Inoculum development typically involves multiple stages of culture scale-up from a seed culture to a production-scale fermenter.
- **Process Parameters and Control:**
    - **Dissolved Oxygen (DO):** Maintaining sufficient DO levels is critical for aerobic fermentation. DO is monitored and controlled by adjusting aeration rate and agitation speed.
    - **Temperature Control:** Maintaining optimal temperature using cooling or heating systems.
    - **pH Control:** Automated pH control systems using pH probes and acid/base addition.
    - **Nutrient Feeding Rate (for Fed-Batch):** Controlled feeding of carbon and nitrogen sources based on monitoring substrate consumption and microbial growth.
    - **Foam Control:** Antifoam agents are added to control foam formation, which can interfere with aeration and cause overflow.
    - **Monitoring and Control Systems:** Online monitoring of parameters like DO, pH, temperature, substrate concentrations, biomass concentration, and protease activity. SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) systems are used for process monitoring and control.

        Fig. Submerged Fermentation (SmF)
        

**Solid-State Fermentation (SSF) for Protease Production:**

- **Substrates:** Solid substrates are used as both nutrient source and support matrix. Commonly used substrates for SSF protease production include:
    - **Agricultural Residues:** Wheat bran, rice bran, corn bran, sugarcane bagasse, soybean meal, oil cakes, straws. These substrates are cost-effective and readily available.
    - **Moistening Agents:** Water, mineral salt solutions, or nutrient solutions are used to moisten the solid substrate to optimal moisture levels for microbial growth.
- **Fermentation Conditions:**
    - **Aerobic Fermentation:** SSF is typically an aerobic process, relying on natural aeration within the solid substrate matrix.
    - **Temperature Control:** Temperature is controlled by managing aeration and heat dissipation from the compost-like bed.
    - **Moisture Control:** Maintaining optimal moisture content is crucial for microbial growth and enzyme production in SSF. Moisture content is controlled by adjusting water content of the substrate and aeration.
    - **pH Control (Less Common):** pH is generally not actively controlled in SSF, but substrate selection and amendments can influence pH.
    - **Inoculation:** Solid substrate is inoculated with spores or mycelia of protease-producing microorganisms, typically fungi like *Aspergillus* or *Trichoderma*.
- **Process Parameters and Control for SSF:**
    - **Moisture Content:** Optimal moisture content for the solid substrate (typically 50-70%).
    - **Aeration:** Maintaining sufficient aeration within the solid bed. Turning or forced aeration may be used.
    - **Temperature:** Temperature control through aeration and management of heat generation.
    - **Substrate Particle Size and Bed Porosity:** Particle size and bulking agents influence bed porosity and aeration.
    - **Incubation Time:** Fermentation time is longer in SSF compared to SmF, typically days to weeks.

**3.1.1.3 Product Recovery and Applications of Protease**

**Product Recovery (Downstream Processing) of Protease:**

Protease recovery from fermentation broth typically involves the following downstream processing steps:

![Fig. Downstream Process](Unit III 1a63a995df878010bfefd5d2faf7d80a/image%203.png)

Fig. Downstream Process

1. **Cell Mass Removal:**
    - **Centrifugation:** Disc-stack centrifugation is commonly used for large-scale removal of cell mass from fermentation broth.
    - **Filtration:** Depth filtration or microfiltration can be used for clarification and removal of finer particles and cell debris.
2. **Product Concentration:**
    - **Ultrafiltration (UF):** UF is used to concentrate the protease enzyme in the clarified broth by removing water and smaller molecules. UF is a gentle and efficient concentration method for proteins.
    - **Precipitation:** Ammonium sulfate precipitation or solvent precipitation can be used for bulk protein concentration.
3. **Purification (Chromatography):** Chromatography techniques are used to further purify the protease enzyme, removing remaining impurities and achieving the desired purity level.
    - **Affinity Chromatography:** If a specific ligand or affinity tag is available for the protease, affinity chromatography can be used for high-selectivity purification.
    - **Ion-Exchange Chromatography (IEX):** IEX is widely used for protease purification, separating proteases based on their charge properties. Cation exchange or anion exchange chromatography can be employed depending on the protease’s isoelectric point and desired binding and elution conditions.
    - **Size-Exclusion Chromatography (SEC) / Gel Filtration Chromatography:** SEC can be used as a polishing step to remove protein aggregates or to separate proteases based on size.
4. **Formulation and Stabilization:**
    - **Drying:** Drying techniques (spray drying, freeze drying) are used to obtain a stable, solid enzyme product. Lyophilization (freeze drying) is often preferred for heat-sensitive proteases to maintain enzyme activity and stability.
    - **Formulation:** Protease preparations are often formulated with stabilizers (e.g., salts, polyols, polymers), enzyme activators, or other additives to enhance enzyme stability, activity, and application properties. Liquid or solid formulations can be prepared depending on the application.

**Applications of Protease Enzymes:**

Proteases have a wide range of industrial applications due to their ability to hydrolyze proteins. Major applications include:

- **Detergents:** Alkaline proteases (subtilisins) are key components of laundry detergents and dishwashing detergents. They aid in removing protein-based stains (blood, grass, food) from fabrics and dishes.
- **Food Industry:**
    - **Baking Industry:** Proteases are used as dough improvers in baking, modifying gluten proteins to improve dough handling, texture, and volume of baked goods.
    - **Meat Tenderization:** Proteases (papain, bromelain, ficin) are used to tenderize meat by breaking down muscle proteins.
    - **Cheese Making:** Rennin (chymosin), an aspartic protease, is used for milk clotting in cheese production. Microbial rennets produced by *Rhizomucor miehei*, *Rhizomucor pusillus*, and *Cryphonectria parasitica* are used as vegetarian alternatives to animal rennet.
    - **Brewing Industry:** Proteases are used to prevent haze formation in beer, improve beer clarity, and modify protein content.
- **Leather Processing:** Proteases are used in leather processing for dehairing, bating (softening leather), and improving leather quality.
- **Textile Industry:** Proteases are used in textile processing for biopolishing (removing fuzz and pills from fabrics), desizing (removing starch coatings), and bio-scouring (removing impurities from cotton).
- **Pharmaceutical Industry:**
    - **Therapeutic Enzymes:** Some proteases (e.g., trypsin, chymotrypsin, bromelain, serratiopeptidase) are used as therapeutic enzymes for wound debridement, anti-inflammatory agents, and digestive aids.
    - **Enzyme-Based Pharmaceuticals:** Proteases are used in the production of certain peptide and protein drugs.
- **Bioremediation:** Proteases can be used in bioremediation applications to degrade proteinaceous waste and organic pollutants.
- **Research and Diagnostics:** Proteases are widely used in research laboratories and diagnostic kits for protein analysis, peptide synthesis, cell lysis, and various biochemical assays.

### 3.1.2 Pectinase

Pectinases are a group of enzymes that catalyze the hydrolysis of pectin, a complex polysaccharide found in plant cell walls, particularly in fruits and vegetables. Pectinases are widely used in the food and beverage industry, especially in fruit juice processing and winemaking.

![Fig. Production of Pectinase](Unit III 1a63a995df878010bfefd5d2faf7d80a/image%204.png)

Fig. Production of Pectinase

**3.1.2.1 Microorganisms Involved in Pectinase Production**

Pectinases are produced by a variety of microorganisms, including bacteria and fungi. Industrially important pectinase-producing microorganisms include:

- **Fungi (Major Industrial Producers):**
    - ***Aspergillus niger*:** *Aspergillus niger* is the most important industrial producer of pectinases. It is a filamentous fungus known for its high enzyme secretion capacity and GRAS status. *Aspergillus niger* produces a complex mixture of pectinolytic enzymes, including polygalacturonases, pectin lyases, and pectin esterases.
    - ***Aspergillus awamori*:** Another *Aspergillus* species used for pectinase production.
    - ***Trichoderma reesei*:** While primarily known for cellulase production, *Trichoderma reesei* also produces pectinases as part of its plant cell wall-degrading enzyme complex.
- **Bacteria (Less Common for Large-Scale Industrial Production Compared to Fungi):**
    - ***Bacillus subtilis*:** Some *Bacillus subtilis* strains produce pectin lyases and other pectinolytic enzymes.
    - ***Bacillus licheniformis*:** Similar to *Bacillus subtilis*, some *Bacillus licheniformis* strains can produce pectinases.
    - ***Erwinia chrysanthemi*:** A bacterium known to produce pectin lyases, particularly pectate lyases.

Fungi, especially *Aspergillus niger*, are preferred for industrial pectinase production due to their high extracellular enzyme secretion levels, GRAS status, and ability to produce a diverse range of pectinolytic enzymes.

**3.1.2.2 Fermentation Process for Pectinase Production**

Pectinase production through fermentation can be carried out using submerged fermentation (SmF) or solid-state fermentation (SSF). SmF is more commonly used for large-scale industrial production.

**Submerged Fermentation (SmF) for Pectinase Production:**

- **Raw Materials/Substrates:**
    - **Carbon Source:** Pectin-rich materials are used as both carbon source and inducer for pectinase production. Examples include:
        - **Citrus Pectin:** Pectin extracted from citrus peels is a common and effective inducer and substrate for pectinase production.
        - **Apple Pomace:** A byproduct of apple juice and cider production, rich in pectin.
        - **Sugar Beet Pulp:** A byproduct of sugar beet processing, containing pectin and other carbohydrates.
        - **Other Pectin-Containing Agricultural Residues:** Fruit and vegetable peels, pomaces, and pulps.
        - **Simple Carbon Sources (for Growth):** Glucose, sucrose, or other sugars may be added as readily utilizable carbon sources to support initial microbial growth before pectin induction.
    - **Nitrogen Source:** Organic nitrogen sources are typically used, such as:
        - **Corn Steep Liquor:** A byproduct of corn wet milling, rich in nitrogen and growth factors.
        - **Yeast Extract:** Provides amino acids, peptides, and vitamins.
        - **Ammonium Salts (Supplementary):** Ammonium sulfate or ammonium nitrate may be added as supplementary inorganic nitrogen sources.
    - **Minerals:** Essential minerals (phosphorus, magnesium, calcium, iron, zinc, manganese) are required for microbial growth and enzyme synthesis.
- **Fermentation Conditions:**
    - **Fermentation Type:** Typically **batch fermentation** or **fed-batch fermentation**. Fed-batch fermentation can be used to optimize substrate feeding and achieve higher pectinase yields.
    - **Aeration:** **Aerobic fermentation**. Pectinase production by *Aspergillus niger* and other industrial fungi is aerobic. Sufficient dissolved oxygen is required for fungal growth and enzyme biosynthesis.
    - **Temperature:** Temperature is controlled within the optimal range for the specific fungal strain, typically in the mesophilic range (25-30°C) for *Aspergillus niger*.
    - **pH:** pH is controlled within the optimal range for fungal growth and pectinase production, typically slightly acidic (pH 4.0-5.5) for *Aspergillus niger*. Acidic pH also helps to minimize bacterial contamination.
    - **Agitation:** Agitation is used to ensure proper mixing, oxygen transfer, and temperature uniformity in the fermenter.
    - **Induction:** Pectin-rich substrates act as inducers for pectinase production. Pectin or pectin-containing materials are included in the fermentation medium to induce pectinase gene expression and enzyme synthesis.
- **Process Parameters and Control:** Similar to protease fermentation, key parameters include DO, temperature, pH, nutrient feeding rate (for fed-batch), foam control, and process monitoring using SCADA systems.

**Solid-State Fermentation (SSF) for Pectinase Production:**

- **Substrates:** Solid substrates rich in pectin are used in SSF for pectinase production. Examples include:
    - **Citrus Peels:** Dried citrus peels are a common and effective substrate for SSF pectinase production.
    - **Apple Pomace:** Apple pomace, dried or semi-dried, can be used as a substrate.
    - **Wheat Bran:** Wheat bran, a byproduct of wheat milling, provides nutrients and structure for fungal growth in SSF.
    - **Mixtures of Substrates:** Combinations of agricultural residues and pectin-rich materials are often used to optimize SSF.
- **Fermentation Conditions:**
    - **Aerobic Fermentation:** SSF is inherently an aerobic process, relying on natural aeration of the solid substrate bed.
    - **Moisture Content:** Optimal moisture content is crucial for fungal growth and enzyme production in SSF (typically 50-70%).
    - **Temperature Control:** Temperature is controlled by managing aeration and heat dissipation from the solid substrate bed.
    - **pH Control (Less Common):** pH is generally not actively controlled in SSF, but substrate selection and amendments can influence pH.
    - **Inoculation:** Solid substrate is inoculated with spores of pectinase-producing fungi, such as *Aspergillus niger*.

**3.1.2.3 Product Recovery and Applications of Pectinase**

**Product Recovery (Downstream Processing) of Pectinase:**

Pectinase recovery from fermentation broth or solid-state fermentation extracts typically involves the following steps:

1. **Solid-Liquid Separation:**
    - **For SmF:** Cell mass removal using centrifugation or filtration to obtain clarified enzyme broth.
    - **For SSF:** Enzyme extraction from the solid fermented substrate using water or buffer extraction. Solid-liquid separation (filtration or centrifugation) to remove solid substrate residues from the crude enzyme extract.
2. **Enzyme Concentration:**
    - **Ultrafiltration (UF):** UF is used to concentrate the pectinase enzyme in the clarified broth or extract.
    - **Precipitation:** Ammonium sulfate precipitation or ethanol precipitation can be used for bulk enzyme concentration, although less common for pectinases compared to UF.
3. **Purification (Chromatography - Less Common for Industrial Pectinases):** For many industrial applications, pectinases are used in crude or partially purified form, and extensive purification is not always necessary. However, for specific applications requiring higher purity, chromatography techniques can be used.
    - **Ion-Exchange Chromatography (IEX):** IEX can be used to fractionate pectinase enzymes based on their charge properties.
    - **Size-Exclusion Chromatography (SEC):** SEC can be used to remove high molecular weight impurities or to fractionate pectinases based on size.
4. **Formulation and Stabilization:**
    - **Enzyme Preparation:** Pectinase preparations are often formulated as liquid enzyme concentrates or as dried enzyme powders.
    - **Stabilization:** Stabilizers (salts, polyols, polymers) may be added to enzyme formulations to enhance enzyme stability and shelf life.

**Applications of Pectinase Enzymes:**

Pectinases have numerous applications, primarily in the food and beverage industry:

- **Fruit Juice Processing:** The largest application of pectinases.
    - **Juice Clarification:** Pectinases are used to clarify fruit juices (apple, citrus, berry juices) by hydrolyzing pectin, reducing viscosity, and removing haze-forming pectin colloids, resulting in clearer and more filterable juices.
    - **Juice Extraction and Yield Improvement:** Pectinases enhance fruit juice extraction from fruit pulp by breaking down pectin in cell walls, increasing juice yield and reducing pressing time.
    - **Viscosity Reduction:** Pectinases reduce the viscosity of fruit purees and concentrates, making them easier to process and handle.
- **Winemaking:**
    - **Wine Clarification:** Pectinases are used to clarify wine by removing pectin haze and improving wine filterability.
    - **Color Extraction:** Pectinases enhance color extraction from grape skins during red wine production by breaking down pectin in grape cell walls, releasing anthocyanin pigments.
    - **Flavor and Aroma Enhancement:** Pectinases can contribute to flavor and aroma development in wine by releasing flavor-active compounds from fruit cell walls.
- **Vegetable Processing:**
    - **Vegetable Juice and Puree Processing:** Pectinases are used to clarify vegetable juices and reduce the viscosity of vegetable purees.
    - **Potato Processing:** Pectinases can be used to improve potato processing, such as potato maceration and texture modification.
- **Plant Fiber Processing:**
    - **Extraction of Plant Fibers:** Pectinases can be used to aid in the extraction of plant fibers (e.g., flax, hemp, ramie) by removing pectin that binds fibers together.
    - **Biopolishing of Cotton Fabrics:** Pectinases, along with other enzymes, can be used for biopolishing of cotton fabrics to improve fabric smoothness and softness.
- **Other Applications:**
    - **Paper and Pulp Industry:** Pectinases are used in paper and pulp processing for bio-bleaching and improving paper properties.
    - **Animal Feed Industry:** Pectinases can be added to animal feed to improve digestibility of plant-based feed materials.
    - **Enzyme-Based Assays and Research:** Pectinases are used in enzyme assays for pectin analysis and in research related to plant cell wall degradation and plant biotechnology.

## 3.2 Organic Acid Production

Organic acids are carboxylic acids that are produced by various microorganisms through fermentation. Several organic acids are industrially important as food additives, preservatives, platform chemicals, and monomers for biopolymers. Microbial fermentation is a major route for the large-scale production of organic acids.

![Fig. Citric Acid Production](Unit III 1a63a995df878010bfefd5d2faf7d80a/image%205.png)

Fig. Citric Acid Production

### 3.2.1 Citric Acid

Citric acid is a tricarboxylic acid that is ubiquitous in nature, found in citrus fruits and produced as an intermediate in the Krebs cycle (citric acid cycle). Citric acid is one of the most important organic acids produced by fermentation, with a wide range of applications in the food, beverage, pharmaceutical, and chemical industries.

**3.2.1.1 Microorganisms Involved in Citric Acid Production**

*Aspergillus niger* is the predominant microorganism used for industrial citric acid production.

- ***Aspergillus niger* (Filamentous Fungus):** *Aspergillus niger* is a filamentous fungus known for its high citric acid production capacity and GRAS status. Specific strains of *Aspergillus niger* have been developed and optimized for industrial citric acid fermentation.

While *Aspergillus niger* is the primary industrial producer, other microorganisms can also produce citric acid, but are less commercially significant:

- **Yeasts:**
    - ***Candida* species:** Some *Candida* yeasts, such as *Candida guilliermondii* and *Candida lipolytica*, can produce citric acid, particularly from *n*alkanes or glycerol as substrates. Yeast fermentation is less common for citric acid production compared to *Aspergillus niger*.
    - ***Yarrowia lipolytica*:** *Yarrowia lipolytica*, a oleaginous yeast, can produce citric acid and other organic acids from various substrates, including glucose and oils.
- **Bacteria:**
    - ***Bacillus subtilis*:** Some *Bacillus subtilis* strains can produce citric acid, but bacterial citric acid production is generally lower than fungal production and less industrially relevant.

**3.2.1.2 Fermentation Process for Citric Acid Production**

Citric acid fermentation is typically carried out using submerged fermentation (SmF) with *Aspergillus niger*.

![Fig. Production of Citric Acid](Unit III 1a63a995df878010bfefd5d2faf7d80a/image%206.png)

Fig. Production of Citric Acid

**Submerged Fermentation (SmF) for Citric Acid Production:**

- **Raw Materials/Substrates:**
    - **Carbon Source:** Carbohydrates are the primary carbon sources for citric acid fermentation.
        - **Molasses (Beet Molasses, Cane Molasses):** A byproduct of sugar beet and sugarcane processing, rich in sucrose and other sugars. Molasses is a cost-effective and widely used carbon source for industrial citric acid production.
        - **Glucose Syrup (Corn Syrup):** Glucose derived from starch hydrolysis. Glucose syrup is a purified and readily metabolized carbon source, often used for high-purity citric acid production.
        - **Sucrose:** Pure sucrose can also be used, although less common due to higher cost compared to molasses or glucose syrup.
        - **Starch-Based Substrates:** Starch hydrolysates or raw starch (e.g., corn starch, cassava starch) can be used, often requiring amylase enzymes to hydrolyze starch to glucose before fermentation.
    - **Nitrogen Source:** Nitrogen is required in limited amounts as citric acid fermentation is typically performed under nitrogen-limiting conditions to promote citric acid overproduction.
        - **Ammonium Salts:** Ammonium nitrate, ammonium sulfate, ammonium chloride are commonly used inorganic nitrogen sources. Nitrogen concentration is carefully controlled to limit biomass growth and favor citric acid accumulation.
    - **Minerals:** Essential minerals are required, including:
        - **Phosphate:** Potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH₂PO₄) or ammonium phosphate.
        - **Magnesium:** Magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄).
        - **Trace Metals:** Iron (Fe), Zinc (Zn), Manganese (Mn), Copper (Cu), Molybdenum (Mo). Trace metal concentrations are critical. Iron and manganese, in particular, are carefully controlled as they can affect citric acid yield and byproduct formation.
    - **Methanol (Optional):** Methanol is sometimes added in small amounts (0.1-1% v/v) to enhance citric acid production in molasses-based fermentation. Methanol’s role is not fully understood but may affect cell membrane permeability or metabolic regulation.
- **Fermentation Conditions:**
    - **Fermentation Type:** Typically **batch fermentation** or **fed-batch fermentation**.
        - **Batch Fermentation:** Simple and widely used for citric acid production. All nutrients are added at the beginning.
        - **Fed-Batch Fermentation:** Carbon source (sugar solution) is fed intermittently or continuously during fermentation to maintain optimal sugar concentrations, avoid catabolite repression, and achieve higher citric acid concentrations and yields. Fed-batch is preferred for high-productivity citric acid fermentation.
    - **Aeration:** **Aerobic fermentation**. Citric acid production by *Aspergillus niger* is strictly aerobic, requiring high dissolved oxygen levels for efficient oxidative metabolism and citric acid synthesis. High aeration rates and efficient oxygen transfer are crucial.
    - **Temperature:** Temperature is controlled within the optimal range for *Aspergillus niger*, typically 28-32°C.
    - **pH:** pH is critically controlled at a low acidic pH, typically between pH 1.5-2.5. Low pH is essential for:
        - **Selective Fermentation:** Low pH inhibits the growth of many contaminating bacteria and yeasts, favoring the growth of *Aspergillus niger* and reducing contamination risks.
        - **Citric Acid Accumulation:** Low pH favors the extracellular accumulation of citric acid in its free acid form, which is less toxic to the fungus and facilitates product recovery. At higher pH, citric acid is ionized and accumulates as citrate salts intracellularly, which can be inhibitory.
    - **Agitation:** Agitation is essential for mixing, oxygen transfer, and temperature uniformity in the fermenter.
    - **Spore Inoculum:** Spores of *Aspergillus niger* are typically used as inoculum. Spore inoculum is prepared in advance and inoculated into the production fermenter.
- **Process Parameters and Control:**
    - **Dissolved Oxygen (DO):** Maintaining high DO levels is crucial. DO is monitored and controlled by adjusting aeration rate and agitation speed.
    - **Temperature Control:** Maintaining optimal temperature using cooling systems.
    - **pH Control:** Automated pH control systems using pH probes and acid/base addition to maintain low acidic pH.
    - **Substrate Concentration Control (for Fed-Batch):** Controlled feeding of carbon source to maintain optimal sugar concentrations and avoid catabolite repression or substrate limitation.
    - **Nitrogen Limitation:** Maintaining nitrogen-limiting conditions to promote citric acid overproduction and limit biomass growth.
    - **Trace Metal Control (Iron, Manganese):** Carefully controlling the concentrations of trace metals like iron and manganese in the medium. Low iron and manganese concentrations are known to promote citric acid accumulation by reducing the activity of aconitase and isocitrate dehydrogenase, enzymes in the Krebs cycle that consume citrate. However, complete metal depletion can inhibit fungal growth. Optimal trace metal concentrations need to be maintained.
    - **Foam Control:** Antifoam agents are added to control foam formation.
    - **Morphology Control:** *Aspergillus niger* morphology (pellet formation vs. dispersed mycelial growth) can affect citric acid production. Morphology can be influenced by agitation, aeration, and medium composition.

**Product Recovery and Applications of Citric Acid**

**Product Recovery (Downstream Processing) of Citric Acid:**

Citric acid is typically recovered from fermentation broth through precipitation, extraction, and crystallization.

![Fig. Citric Acid Selective Recovery](Unit III 1a63a995df878010bfefd5d2faf7d80a/image%207.png)

Fig. Citric Acid Selective Recovery

1. **Cell Mass Removal:**
    - **Filtration:** Rotary vacuum filtration or pressure filtration is commonly used to remove fungal biomass (*Aspergillus niger* mycelia) from the fermentation broth.
2. **Citric Acid Precipitation as Calcium Citrate:**
    - **Lime Precipitation:** Calcium hydroxide (lime, Ca(OH)₂) is added to the clarified fermentation broth to precipitate citric acid as insoluble calcium citrate. Calcium citrate precipitation is performed at elevated temperatures (e.g., 70-90°C) to improve precipitate purity and settling.
    - **Washing Calcium Citrate:** Calcium citrate precipitate is separated by filtration or centrifugation and washed with water to remove impurities.
3. **Citric Acid Regeneration and Purification:**
    - **Sulfuric Acid Treatment:** Calcium citrate precipitate is treated with sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) to regenerate citric acid and precipitate insoluble calcium sulfate (gypsum).
    - **Filtration of Calcium Sulfate:** Calcium sulfate precipitate is removed by filtration. The filtrate contains crude citric acid solution.
    - **Activated Carbon Treatment (Optional):** The crude citric acid solution may be treated with activated carbon to remove color and organic impurities.
4. **Concentration and Crystallization of Citric Acid:**
    - **Evaporation:** Citric acid solution is concentrated by evaporation under vacuum to achieve supersaturation.
    - **Crystallization:** Citric acid is crystallized from the concentrated solution by cooling crystallization. Seeding with citric acid crystals is often used to promote controlled crystallization.
    - **Centrifugation and Drying:** Citric acid crystals are separated by centrifugation and dried to obtain the final crystalline citric acid product.

**Applications of Citric Acid:**

Citric acid has a vast range of applications due to its properties as a food acidulant, preservative, flavoring agent, chelating agent, and buffer. Major applications include:

- **Food and Beverage Industry (Acidulant, Flavoring Agent, Preservative):** The largest application of citric acid.
    - **Acidulant in Beverages:** Used to impart a tart taste and enhance flavor in carbonated drinks, fruit juices, and other beverages.
    - **Preservative in Food:** Acts as a food preservative due to its acidity and antioxidant properties, inhibiting microbial growth and preventing spoilage in jams, jellies, candies, and processed foods.
    - **Flavoring Agent:** Used as a flavoring agent in candies, desserts, and fruit-flavored products.
    - **Synergist for Antioxidants:** Enhances the effectiveness of antioxidants like ascorbic acid (Vitamin C).
- **Pharmaceutical Industry (Acidulant, Antioxidant, Stabilizer, Effervescent Formulations):**
    - **Acidulant in Pharmaceuticals:** Used to adjust pH and improve the taste of pharmaceutical formulations.
    - **Anticoagulant in Blood Products:** Citric acid salts (sodium citrate) are used as anticoagulants in blood collection and storage to prevent blood clotting.
    - **Effervescent Tablets and Powders:** Citric acid, in combination with bicarbonates, is used to produce effervescent tablets and powders that dissolve with fizzing action when added to water.
    - **Stabilizer and Antioxidant:** Used as a stabilizer and antioxidant in some pharmaceutical formulations.
- **Chemical Industry (Chelating Agent, Cleaning Agent, Industrial Applications):**
    - **Chelating Agent:** Citric acid is a strong chelating agent, capable of binding metal ions. Used in detergents, cleaning products, and industrial cleaning to remove scale, rust, and metal ions.
    - **Builder in Detergents and Soaps:** Used as a builder in detergents and soaps to enhance cleaning action and water softening.
    - **Industrial Cleaning and Metal Treatment:** Used for cleaning industrial equipment, boilers, and metal surfaces, and in metal plating and finishing processes.
    - **Plasticizer and Polymer Modifier:** Citric acid esters are used as non-toxic plasticizers for polymers and as modifiers for biodegradable polymers.
- **Cosmetics and Personal Care Products (pH Adjuster, Antioxidant):**
    - **pH Adjuster:** Used to adjust the pH of cosmetics and personal care products to be skin-friendly.
    - **Antioxidant:** Acts as an antioxidant in some cosmetic formulations.

## 3.3 Antibiotic Production

Antibiotics are secondary metabolites produced by microorganisms that inhibit or kill other microorganisms, particularly bacteria. Antibiotics are of immense medical importance for treating bacterial infections. Fermentation is the primary method for the large-scale production of antibiotics.

### 3.3.1 Penicillin

Penicillin, discovered by **Alexander Fleming** in 1928 and developed for large-scale production by **Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, and Norman Heatley** in the 1940s, is one of the most important and widely used antibiotics. Penicillin is a β-lactam antibiotic that inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis.

**3.3.1.1 Microorganisms Involved in Penicillin Production**

*Penicillium chrysogenum* is the primary microorganism used for the industrial production of penicillin.

- ***Penicillium chrysogenum* (Filamentous Fungus):** *Penicillium chrysogenum* is a filamentous fungus belonging to the genus *Penicillium*. Specific high-yielding strains of *Penicillium chrysogenum* have been developed through strain improvement programs for industrial penicillin fermentation.

Historically, *Penicillium notatum* was the species from which Fleming first discovered penicillin, but *Penicillium chrysogenum* strains are superior producers and are used for industrial production.

**3.3.1.2 Fermentation Process for Penicillin Production**

Penicillin fermentation is typically carried out using submerged fermentation (SmF) in large-scale stirred tank bioreactors. Penicillin production is a **secondary metabolite fermentation**, meaning that penicillin is not produced during the rapid growth phase (trophophase) but is synthesized primarily during the stationary phase (idiophase) when growth slows down due to nutrient limitation.

![Fig. Penicillin Production ](Unit III 1a63a995df878010bfefd5d2faf7d80a/image%208.png)

Fig. Penicillin Production 

**Submerged Fermentation (SmF) for Penicillin Production:**

- **Raw Materials/Substrates:**
    - **Carbon Source:**
        - **Glucose:** Glucose was initially used as a carbon source but is now less common due to catabolite repression of penicillin biosynthesis.
        - **Lactose:** Lactose is the preferred carbon source for industrial penicillin fermentation. Lactose is a slowly metabolized disaccharide that avoids strong catabolite repression and provides a sustained carbon source for penicillin production during the idiophase.
        - **Corn Steep Liquor:** Corn steep liquor, a byproduct of corn wet milling, is a complex nutrient source that also contains carbohydrates and acts as a carbon source, as well as providing nitrogen and growth factors.
    - **Nitrogen Source:** Organic nitrogen sources are important for penicillin production.
        - **Corn Steep Liquor:** Corn steep liquor is a major nitrogen source and provides amino acids, peptides, and vitamins that are beneficial for penicillin biosynthesis.
        - **Phenylacetic Acid (PAA) or Phenoxyacetic Acid (POA):** Precursors for penicillin side chain. Phenylacetic acid is a precursor for penicillin G (benzylpenicillin), and phenoxyacetic acid is a precursor for penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin). The choice of precursor determines the type of penicillin produced. Precursors are added to the fermentation medium to direct penicillin biosynthesis towards the desired penicillin type.
    - **Minerals:** Essential minerals are required for fungal growth and penicillin biosynthesis.
        - **Phosphate:** Phosphate concentration is carefully controlled as high phosphate levels can repress penicillin biosynthesis. Phosphate limitation is often used as a strategy to promote penicillin production during the idiophase.
        - **Sulfate:** Sulfate is required for penicillin biosynthesis.
        - **Precursors:** Phenylacetic acid (for Penicillin G) or Phenoxyacetic acid (for Penicillin V).
    - **Precursor Feeding:** Precursors (PAA or POA) are typically fed intermittently or continuously during the fermentation to control their concentration and avoid toxicity while ensuring sufficient precursor supply for penicillin biosynthesis.
- **Fermentation Conditions:**
    - **Fermentation Type:** **Fed-batch fermentation** is the predominant fermentation mode for industrial penicillin production. Fed-batch operation allows for controlled feeding of carbon source and precursors, avoiding catabolite repression, maintaining optimal substrate concentrations, and achieving high penicillin titers.
    - **Aeration:** **Aerobic fermentation**. Penicillin biosynthesis by *Penicillium chrysogenum* is strictly aerobic, requiring high dissolved oxygen levels. Efficient aeration and oxygen transfer are crucial for high penicillin production.
    - **Temperature:** Temperature is controlled within the optimal range for *Penicillium chrysogenum*, typically 25-27°C.
    - **pH:** pH is controlled within a slightly acidic to neutral range, typically around pH 6.0-6.5 during the production phase. pH control is important for fungal growth and penicillin biosynthesis.
    - **Agitation:** Agitation is used to ensure proper mixing, oxygen transfer, and temperature uniformity. Agitation intensity is optimized to balance mixing and aeration with shear sensitivity of *Penicillium chrysogenum* mycelia.
    - **Morphology Control:** *Penicillium chrysogenum* morphology (pellet formation vs. dispersed mycelial growth) can affect penicillin production. Morphology can be influenced by agitation, aeration, and medium composition. Dispersed mycelial growth is generally preferred for penicillin production as it enhances nutrient uptake and oxygen transfer.
- **Process Parameters and Control:**
    - **Dissolved Oxygen (DO):** Maintaining high DO levels is critical. DO is monitored and controlled by adjusting aeration rate and agitation speed.
    - **Temperature Control:** Maintaining optimal temperature using cooling systems.
    - **pH Control:** Automated pH control systems using pH probes and acid/base addition to maintain slightly acidic to neutral pH.
    - **Substrate Feeding Rate (for Fed-Batch):** Controlled feeding of carbon source (lactose or glucose) and precursors (PAA or POA) based on monitoring substrate consumption, biomass growth, and penicillin production rate.
    - **Phosphate Limitation:** Maintaining phosphate-limiting conditions during the production phase to promote penicillin biosynthesis and reduce biomass growth.
    - **Precursor Feeding Strategy:** Optimized feeding strategy for precursors (PAA or POA) to maximize penicillin production and minimize precursor toxicity or byproduct formation.
    - **Foam Control:** Antifoam agents are added to control foam formation.
    - **Process Monitoring and Control Software:** SCADA systems for real-time monitoring and control of fermentation parameters.

**3.3.1.3 Product Recovery and Applications of Penicillin**

**Product Recovery (Downstream Processing) of Penicillin:**

Penicillin is typically recovered from fermentation broth through a combination of solvent extraction, precipitation, and chromatography. Penicillin is produced extracellularly, so cell mass removal is the first step.

1. **Cell Mass Removal:**
    - **Filtration:** Rotary vacuum filtration or pressure filtration is used to remove *Penicillium chrysogenum* mycelia from the fermentation broth.
2. **Solvent Extraction:** Penicillin is extracted from the clarified fermentation broth using liquid-liquid extraction with an organic solvent.
    - **pH Adjustment:** The pH of the broth is adjusted to acidic pH (e.g., pH 2-3) to convert penicillin salts (which are more soluble in water) to the free acid form, which is more soluble in organic solvents.
    - **Solvent Extraction:** Extraction is typically performed using a water-immiscible organic solvent, such as butyl acetate or methyl isobutyl ketone (MIBK). Countercurrent extraction systems are used for large-scale industrial extraction. Penicillin partitions preferentially into the organic solvent phase.
    - **Multiple Extraction Stages:** Multiple extraction stages are often used to improve penicillin recovery efficiency.
3. **Back Extraction into Aqueous Phase:** Penicillin is back-extracted from the organic solvent phase into an aqueous phase at a higher pH (e.g., pH 7-8) to convert penicillin free acid back to a water-soluble salt form (e.g., potassium or sodium penicillin salt). Back extraction concentrates penicillin in a smaller volume of aqueous solution.
4. **Precipitation of Penicillin Salt:** Penicillin is precipitated from the concentrated aqueous solution as a crystalline salt.
    - **Potassium Penicillin G Crystallization:** Potassium penicillin G is commonly crystallized by adding potassium salts (e.g., potassium acetate) to the concentrated penicillin solution. Crystallization is performed at low temperatures to improve crystal quality and yield.
    - **Filtration and Washing of Crystals:** Penicillin crystals are separated by filtration and washed to remove impurities.
5. **Drying and Formulation:**
    - **Drying:** Penicillin crystals are dried to obtain a stable, solid penicillin product. Vacuum drying or air drying can be used.
    - **Formulation:** Penicillin is formulated into various pharmaceutical dosage forms, such as powders for injection, tablets, capsules, and oral suspensions, depending on the intended route of administration and therapeutic use. Formulations may include excipients, stabilizers, and buffers.

**Applications of Penicillin Antibiotics:**

Penicillins are broad-spectrum antibiotics effective against a wide range of Gram-positive and some Gram-negative bacteria. They are used to treat various bacterial infections, including:

- **Respiratory Tract Infections:** Pneumonia, bronchitis, tonsillitis, sinusitis.
- **Skin and Soft Tissue Infections:** Cellulitis, impetigo, wound infections.
- **Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs):** Cystitis, pyelonephritis.
- **Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs):** Syphilis, gonorrhea (some strains are resistant).
- **Meningitis:** Bacterial meningitis caused by susceptible bacteria.
- **Surgical Prophylaxis:** To prevent infections before, during, and after surgery.
- **Veterinary Medicine:** Treatment of bacterial infections in animals.

Penicillin and its derivatives (semisynthetic penicillins) remain among the most important and widely used antibiotics globally, although antibiotic resistance is a growing concern.

### 3.3.2 Erythromycin

Erythromycin is a macrolide antibiotic produced by *Saccharopolyspora erythraea* (formerly *Streptomyces erythraeus*). Erythromycin inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 50S ribosomal subunit and is effective against a range of Gram-positive bacteria and some Gram-negative bacteria and atypical pathogens.

![Fig. Erythromycin Production](Unit III 1a63a995df878010bfefd5d2faf7d80a/image%209.png)

Fig. Erythromycin Production

**3.3.2.1 Microorganisms Involved in Erythromycin Production**

*Saccharopolyspora erythraea* is the primary microorganism used for industrial erythromycin production.

- ***Saccharopolyspora erythraea* (Actinomycete Bacterium):** *Saccharopolyspora erythraea* is a filamentous, Gram-positive bacterium belonging to the Actinobacteria class. It is a soil bacterium and a natural producer of erythromycin and related macrolide antibiotics. High-yielding strains of *Saccharopolyspora erythraea* have been developed through strain improvement programs for industrial erythromycin fermentation.

**3.3.2.2 Fermentation Process for Erythromycin Production**

Erythromycin fermentation is typically carried out using submerged fermentation (SmF) in large-scale stirred tank bioreactors. Erythromycin production is also a secondary metabolite fermentation.

**Submerged Fermentation (SmF) for Erythromycin Production:**

- **Raw Materials/Substrates:**
    - **Carbon Source:**
        - **Glucose:** Glucose is a common and readily metabolized carbon source for erythromycin fermentation.
        - **Sucrose:** Sucrose can also be used as a carbon source.
        - **Glycerol:** Glycerol, a byproduct of biodiesel production, is explored as a cost-effective carbon source.
        - **Soybean Oil or Vegetable Oils:** Oils can be used as supplementary carbon sources, particularly in later stages of fermentation to promote erythromycin production.
    - **Nitrogen Source:** Organic and inorganic nitrogen sources are used.
        - **Soybean Flour or Soybean Meal:** Soybean flour or meal is a complex organic nitrogen source that provides amino acids, peptides, and lipids.
        - **Peptone:** Peptone, a protein hydrolysate, is another organic nitrogen source.
        - **Ammonium Salts:** Ammonium sulfate or ammonium nitrate can be used as supplementary inorganic nitrogen sources.
    - **Minerals:** Essential minerals are required for actinomycete growth and erythromycin biosynthesis.
        - **Phosphate:** Phosphate concentration is controlled.
        - **Magnesium:** Magnesium sulfate.
        - **Trace Metals:** Iron, Zinc, Manganese, Copper, Cobalt. Trace metal concentrations are important for enzyme activity and fermentation performance.
- **Fermentation Conditions:**
    - **Fermentation Type:** **Fed-batch fermentation** is the commonly used mode for industrial erythromycin production. Fed-batch operation allows for controlled feeding of carbon source and other nutrients, avoiding catabolite repression, maintaining optimal substrate concentrations, and achieving high erythromycin titers.
    - **Aeration:** **Aerobic fermentation**. Erythromycin biosynthesis by *Saccharopolyspora erythraea* is strictly aerobic. High dissolved oxygen levels are crucial for efficient fermentation.
    - **Temperature:** Temperature is controlled within the optimal range for *Saccharopolyspora erythraea*, typically 28-32°C.
    - **pH:** pH is controlled within a slightly acidic to neutral range, typically around pH 6.5-7.5. pH control is important for microbial growth and erythromycin biosynthesis.
    - **Agitation:** Agitation is used to ensure proper mixing, oxygen transfer, and temperature uniformity. *Saccharopolyspora erythraea* is filamentous, and agitation is optimized to balance mixing and aeration with shear sensitivity of mycelia.
    - **Precursor Feeding (Optional):** While erythromycin biosynthesis does not require specific precursors in the same way as penicillin, feeding with certain amino acids or intermediates may be explored to enhance production in some cases.
- **Process Parameters and Control:** Similar to penicillin fermentation, key parameters include DO, temperature, pH, nutrient feeding rate (for fed-batch), foam control, and process monitoring using SCADA systems.

**3.3.2.3 Product Recovery and Applications of Erythromycin**

**Product Recovery (Downstream Processing) of Erythromycin:**

Erythromycin recovery from fermentation broth typically involves solvent extraction, precipitation, and chromatography. Erythromycin is produced intracellularly and extracellularly, with a significant portion accumulated intracellularly in *Saccharopolyspora erythraea* mycelia.

1. **Cell Mass Removal:**
    - **Centrifugation or Filtration:** Cell mass (*Saccharopolyspora erythraea* mycelia) is removed from the fermentation broth using centrifugation or filtration. Both intracellular and extracellular erythromycin need to be recovered.
2. **Erythromycin Extraction (Solvent Extraction):** Erythromycin is extracted from both the clarified fermentation broth (extracellular erythromycin) and the cell mass (intracellular erythromycin) using solvent extraction.
    - **Solvent Extraction from Fermentation Broth:** The clarified fermentation broth is extracted with an organic solvent, such as butyl acetate, ethyl acetate, or methylene chloride, at alkaline pH (e.g., pH 9-10). Erythromycin base is more soluble in organic solvents at alkaline pH. Countercurrent extraction systems are used for large-scale extraction.
    - **Extraction from Cell Mass:** The cell mass pellet is resuspended in an aqueous solution and then extracted with an organic solvent, similar to the broth extraction, to recover intracellular erythromycin. Cell disruption (e.g., homogenization) may be used before solvent extraction to enhance intracellular erythromycin release.
3. **Back Extraction into Aqueous Phase:** Erythromycin is back-extracted from the organic solvent phase into an aqueous phase at acidic pH (e.g., pH 2-3) to convert erythromycin base back to a water-soluble salt form (e.g., erythromycin hydrochloride or erythromycin lactobionate).
4. **Precipitation and Crystallization of Erythromycin Salt:** Erythromycin salt is precipitated from the concentrated aqueous solution.
    - **Erythromycin Salt Precipitation:** Erythromycin is typically precipitated as erythromycin hydrochloride, erythromycin lactobionate, or other salt forms by adjusting pH, adding counterions, or changing solvent conditions.
    - **Crystallization:** Erythromycin salts can be crystallized to obtain a purified crystalline product. Crystallization conditions are optimized to control crystal size and morphology.
    - **Filtration and Washing of Crystals:** Erythromycin crystals are separated by filtration and washed to remove impurities.
5. **Drying and Formulation:**
    - **Drying:** Erythromycin crystals are dried to obtain a stable, solid erythromycin product. Vacuum drying or air drying can be used.
    - **Formulation:** Erythromycin is formulated into various pharmaceutical dosage forms, such as tablets, capsules, oral suspensions, and intravenous injections. Erythromycin base is poorly water-soluble and is often formulated as water-soluble salts or derivatives (e.g., erythromycin lactobionate, erythromycin ethylsuccinate) for improved bioavailability and administration.

**Applications of Erythromycin Antibiotics:**

Erythromycin is a macrolide antibiotic used to treat a variety of bacterial infections, particularly in patients allergic to penicillin or for infections caused by penicillin-resistant bacteria. Major applications include:

- **Respiratory Tract Infections:** Pneumonia (including atypical pneumonia caused by *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* and *Legionella pneumophila*), bronchitis, whooping cough, diphtheria.
- **Skin and Soft Tissue Infections:** Skin infections, cellulitis, erysipelas, acne.
- **Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs):** Chlamydia infections, gonorrhea (alternative to penicillin in allergic patients).
- **Gastrointestinal Infections:** *Campylobacter jejuni* enteritis.
- **Ocular Infections:** Conjunctivitis.
- **Prophylaxis:** Prevention of bacterial endocarditis in patients undergoing dental procedures who are allergic to penicillin.

Erythromycin and its derivatives (e.g., azithromycin, clarithromycin) are important macrolide antibiotics widely used in clinical medicine. However, macrolide resistance is also an increasing concern, necessitating judicious use and development of new antibiotics.

## 3.4 Vitamin Production

Vitamins are essential organic micronutrients that are required in small amounts for various metabolic and physiological functions in humans and animals. Certain vitamins, particularly some B vitamins and vitamin C, are produced industrially through microbial fermentation. Fermentation offers a sustainable and efficient route for vitamin production compared to chemical synthesis or extraction from natural sources for certain vitamins.

### 3.4.1 Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin)

Vitamin B12, also known as cobalamin, is a complex organometallic compound containing cobalt. It is an essential vitamin required for various metabolic processes, including DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation, and nerve function. Vitamin B12 is not synthesized by plants or animals; microorganisms are the primary producers of vitamin B12 in nature. Industrial production of vitamin B12 relies on microbial fermentation.

Fig. Vitamin B12 Production

**3.4.1.1 Microorganisms Involved in Vitamin B12 Production**

Several microorganisms are capable of producing vitamin B12, including bacteria and archaea. Industrially important vitamin B12-producing microorganisms include:

- **Bacteria (Historically Important, Less Used in Modern Industrial Production):**
    - ***Propionibacterium freudenreichii* and *Propionibacterium shermanii*:** Propionibacteria were among the first microorganisms used for commercial vitamin B12 production. They produce vitamin B12 during anaerobic fermentation of lactic acid or carbohydrates. *Propionibacterium shermanii* was historically significant but is less used now compared to *Pseudomonas* and *Bacillus* for large-scale production.
- **Pseudomonas denitrificans (Currently Predominant Industrial Producer):** *Pseudomonas denitrificans* is a Gram-negative, facultative anaerobic bacterium that is now the primary microorganism used for industrial vitamin B12 production. *Pseudomonas denitrificans* can produce high yields of vitamin B12 under optimized aerobic or anaerobic fermentation conditions.
- **Bacillus megaterium (Also Used Industrially):** *Bacillus megaterium* is a Gram-positive, spore-forming bacterium that is also used for industrial vitamin B12 production, often in co-fermentation or two-stage fermentation systems with other microorganisms.

*Pseudomonas denitrificans* is currently favored for industrial production due to its higher vitamin B12 yields, faster growth rates, and ease of genetic manipulation compared to *Propionibacterium* strains.

**3.4.1.2 Fermentation Process for Vitamin B12 Production**

Vitamin B12 fermentation is a complex, multi-stage process typically carried out using submerged fermentation (SmF). Both aerobic and anaerobic fermentation strategies are employed, often in combination.

**Fermentation Process Outline (General Approach - Hybrid Aerobic/Anaerobic):**

Vitamin B12 fermentation is often optimized using a two-stage or hybrid aerobic-anaerobic process:

1. **Aerobic Growth Phase (Biomass Production - Trophophase):**
    - **Microorganism:** *Pseudomonas denitrificans* or *Bacillus megaterium* (or co-culture).
    - **Fermentation Type:** Aerobic submerged fermentation (SmF).
    - **Carbon Source:** Glucose, sucrose, or molasses.
    - **Nitrogen Source:** Corn steep liquor, yeast extract, ammonium salts.
    - **Cobalt Source:** Cobalt chloride (CoCl₂) or cobalt sulfate (CoSO₄) is added as a precursor for the corrin ring of vitamin B12. Cobalt is an essential and often limiting nutrient for vitamin B12 biosynthesis.
    - **Other Precursors (Optional):** Betaine, 5,6-dimethylbenzimidazole (DMB) or its precursors may be added to enhance vitamin B12 production, depending on the strain and fermentation strategy. DMB is a key precursor for the lower ligand of vitamin B12.
    - **Aerobic Conditions:** High aeration rates to support rapid biomass growth.
    - **pH and Temperature Control:** Optimal pH and temperature for microbial growth.
    - **Purpose:** To rapidly grow a high biomass of the vitamin B12-producing microorganism.
2. **Anaerobic Production Phase (Vitamin B12 Synthesis - Idiophase):**
    - **Transition to Anaerobic Conditions:** After sufficient biomass accumulation in the aerobic growth phase, aeration is reduced or eliminated to create anaerobic or microaerophilic conditions.
    - **Substrate Feeding (Feed-Batch):** Carbon source (glucose, molasses) and cobalt source are fed in a controlled manner to support vitamin B12 biosynthesis during the anaerobic phase.
    - **Precursor Feeding (DMB or its Precursors):** 5,6-Dimethylbenzimidazole (DMB) or its precursors are typically added or fed during the anaerobic production phase. DMB is often a limiting precursor for vitamin B12 biosynthesis, and its addition can significantly enhance vitamin B12 yields.
    - **Anaerobic or Microaerophilic Conditions:** Oxygen levels are reduced to promote anaerobic pathways and vitamin B12 synthesis. Some processes may use microaerophilic conditions (low oxygen levels) for optimized production.
    - **Reductants (Optional):** In some anaerobic processes, reductants or reducing agents may be added to enhance anaerobic conditions and vitamin B12 biosynthesis.
    - **pH and Temperature Control:** pH and temperature are maintained within optimal ranges for anaerobic metabolism and vitamin B12 synthesis.
    - **Purpose:** To induce and maximize vitamin B12 biosynthesis in the stationary phase under anaerobic or microaerophilic conditions.

**Variations in Fermentation Processes:**

- **Aerobic Fermentation:** Some processes use strictly aerobic fermentation for vitamin B12 production, without a distinct anaerobic phase. Aerobic processes may be simpler to operate in terms of oxygen control but may have lower yields compared to optimized anaerobic or hybrid processes.
- **Co-Fermentation or Two-Stage Fermentation:** Some processes utilize co-fermentation with two or more microorganisms or two-stage fermentation with different microorganisms in separate reactors. For example, *Bacillus megaterium* may be used in the first stage for biomass production and precursor synthesis, followed by *Pseudomonas denitrificans* in the second stage for vitamin B12 synthesis, or *Propionibacterium* and *Pseudomonas* co-cultures. Co-fermentation/two-stage fermentation can optimize different aspects of the process and improve overall yields.
- **Genetic Engineering for Strain Improvement:** Genetically engineered strains of *Pseudomonas denitrificans* and other microorganisms with enhanced vitamin B12 biosynthesis pathways, improved precursor utilization, reduced byproduct formation, and better fermentation performance are continuously being developed and used in industrial production.

**Process Parameters and Control for Vitamin B12 Fermentation:**

- **Dissolved Oxygen (DO):** Controlled aeration for aerobic growth phase, and reduced or anaerobic conditions for production phase. DO is monitored and controlled carefully.
- **Temperature Control:** Maintaining optimal temperature for each phase (growth and production).
- **pH Control:** Maintaining optimal pH for each phase.
- **Substrate Feeding Rate (for Fed-Batch):** Controlled feeding of carbon source, cobalt, and DMB precursors.
- **Cobalt Concentration:** Careful control of cobalt concentration in the medium. Cobalt is essential but can be toxic at high concentrations.
- **DMB or Precursor Feeding:** Optimized feeding strategy for DMB or its precursors to maximize vitamin B12 production.
- **Redox Potential (for Anaerobic Phase):** Monitoring and controlling redox potential to ensure anaerobic conditions for the production phase.
- **Precursor Addition Timing:** DMB or precursor addition is typically timed to coincide with the onset of the stationary phase or anaerobic production phase for optimal incorporation into vitamin B12.
- **Foam Control:** Antifoam agents are added to control foam formation.
- **Process Monitoring and Control Software:** SCADA systems for real-time monitoring and control of fermentation parameters.

**3.4.1.3 Product Recovery and Applications of Vitamin B12**

**Product Recovery (Downstream Processing) of Vitamin B12:**

Vitamin B12 recovery from fermentation broth is complex and involves multiple steps to separate, purify, and concentrate the vitamin from the fermentation biomass and broth. Vitamin B12 is primarily intracellular in *Pseudomonas denitrificans* and *Bacillus megaterium*.

1. **Cell Mass Removal:**
    - **Centrifugation:** Cell mass (*Pseudomonas denitrificans* or *Bacillus megaterium*) is removed from the fermentation broth using centrifugation.
2. **Cell Disruption (Cell Lysis):** Cell disruption is necessary to release intracellular vitamin B12 from the microbial cells.
    - **Heat Treatment:** Heating cell suspension to elevated temperatures (e.g., 80-100°C) can induce cell lysis and release vitamin B12. Heat treatment also helps to inactivate enzymes that might degrade vitamin B12.
    - **Chemical Lysis:** Alkali lysis (using NaOH) or lysis with detergents (e.g., SDS) can be used to disrupt cells.
    - **Enzymatic Lysis:** Autolysis or enzymatic lysis using lysozyme or other cell wall-degrading enzymes can be used for milder cell disruption.
3. **Vitamin B12 Extraction:** Vitamin B12 is extracted from the cell lysate using solvent extraction or adsorption techniques.
    - **Solvent Extraction:** Solvent extraction with organic solvents, such as chloroform, phenol, or cresol, is used to extract vitamin B12 from the aqueous cell lysate. Solvent extraction is often performed multiple times to improve recovery efficiency.
    - **Adsorption Chromatography:** Adsorption chromatography using activated carbon or resin adsorbents can be used to adsorb vitamin B12 from the cell lysate. Vitamin B12 is then eluted from the adsorbent using a suitable eluent (e.g., ethanol or acetone).
4. **Purification (Chromatography):** Further purification of vitamin B12 extract is achieved using chromatography techniques.
    - **Phenol Extraction and Chromatography:** A traditional purification method involves phenol extraction followed by column chromatography using ion-exchange chromatography or adsorption chromatography.
    - **High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC):** Preparative HPLC can be used for high-resolution purification of vitamin B12, particularly for pharmaceutical-grade vitamin B12. Reversed-phase HPLC (RP-HPLC) is commonly used.
5. **Crystallization of Vitamin B12:** Vitamin B12 is crystallized from the purified solution to obtain a solid, crystalline vitamin B12 product.
    - **Crystallization Techniques:** Crystallization is typically achieved by adjusting solvent composition, temperature, and pH to reduce vitamin B12 solubility and induce crystallization.
    - **Drying:** Vitamin B12 crystals are separated by filtration or centrifugation and dried under vacuum to obtain the final crystalline vitamin B12 product.

**Applications of Vitamin B12:**

Vitamin B12 is an essential vitamin with critical roles in human and animal health. Major applications include:

- **Nutritional Supplement and Food Fortification:** The primary application of vitamin B12.
    - **Dietary Supplement:** Vitamin B12 is used as a dietary supplement, particularly for vegans and vegetarians who may have limited vitamin B12 intake from plant-based diets, and for individuals with vitamin B12 deficiency due to malabsorption or other medical conditions.
    - **Food Fortification:** Vitamin B12 is used to fortify foods, such as breakfast cereals, plant-based milk alternatives, and nutritional bars, to increase vitamin B12 intake in the population.
- **Pharmaceutical Applications:**
    - **Treatment of Vitamin B12 Deficiency:** Vitamin B12 injections or oral supplements are used to treat vitamin B12 deficiency (cobalamin deficiency), which can lead to megaloblastic anemia, neurological damage, and other health problems.
    - **Treatment of Pernicious Anemia:** Pernicious anemia, caused by impaired vitamin B12 absorption due to lack of intrinsic factor, is treated with vitamin B12 injections.
    - **Neurological Disorders:** Vitamin B12 is sometimes used in the treatment of certain neurological disorders, although its efficacy in some of these conditions is still under investigation.
- **Animal Feed Additive:** Vitamin B12 is added to animal feed, particularly for poultry and swine, to improve animal growth, health, and feed efficiency.
- **Diagnostic and Research Applications:** Vitamin B12 is used in diagnostic assays and biochemical research related to vitamin B12 metabolism and its role in health and disease.

### 3.4.2 Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin)

Vitamin B2, also known as riboflavin, is a water-soluble vitamin that is essential for various metabolic processes, acting as a precursor for the coenzymes flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) and flavin mononucleotide (FMN), which are involved in redox reactions in energy metabolism, cellular respiration, and biosynthesis. Vitamin B2 is produced industrially through microbial fermentation and chemical synthesis, with fermentation being the more sustainable and increasingly preferred method.

**3.4.2.1 Microorganisms Involved in Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin) Production**

Several microorganisms are capable of producing riboflavin, including bacteria, yeasts, and filamentous fungi. Industrially important riboflavin-producing microorganisms include:

- **Fungi (Historically Important, Less Used in Modern Industrial Production):**
    - ***Ashbya gossypii* (Filamentous Fungus):** *Ashbya gossypii*, a filamentous fungus, was historically significant as a high-yielding riboflavin producer and was used in early industrial riboflavin fermentation processes. However, due to morphological issues (filamentous growth, high viscosity) and other limitations, *Ashbya gossypii* is less used in modern large-scale production compared to *Bacillus subtilis* and *Eremothecium ashbyii*.
    - ***Eremothecium ashbyii* (Filamentous Fungus):** *Eremothecium ashbyii*, another filamentous fungus, is also a high-yielding riboflavin producer and is still used for industrial riboflavin production in some regions. It is known for its high riboflavin production capacity and relatively simple fermentation process.
- **Bacteria (Predominant Modern Industrial Producers):**
    - ***Bacillus subtilis* (Gram-Positive Bacterium):** *Bacillus subtilis* is now the predominant microorganism used for industrial riboflavin production. Genetically engineered strains of *Bacillus subtilis* have been developed to achieve very high riboflavin yields. *Bacillus subtilis* offers advantages in terms of genetic manipulability, fast growth rates, and ease of fermentation scale-up.
    - ***Corynebacterium ammoniagenes* (Gram-Positive Bacterium):** *Corynebacterium ammoniagenes*, a Gram-positive bacterium, is also used for riboflavin production, although less common than *Bacillus subtilis*.

*Bacillus subtilis* is currently the most favored microorganism for industrial riboflavin production due to its high yield potential, genetic tractability, and well-established fermentation technology.

**3.4.2.2 Fermentation Process for Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin) Production**

Riboflavin fermentation is typically carried out using submerged fermentation (SmF), primarily with *Bacillus subtilis* strains.

**Submerged Fermentation (SmF) for Riboflavin Production:**

- **Raw Materials/Substrates:**
    - **Carbon Source:**
        - **Glucose:** Glucose is a common and readily metabolized carbon source for riboflavin fermentation.
        - **Molasses:** Molasses (beet or cane molasses) can be used as a cost-effective carbon source, although may require pre-treatment to remove impurities.
        - **Vegetable Oils:** Vegetable oils (soybean oil, corn oil) can be used as supplementary carbon sources or as primary carbon sources in some processes, particularly for *Eremothecium ashbyii* fermentation.
    - **Nitrogen Source:** Organic and inorganic nitrogen sources are used.
        - **Yeast Extract:** Yeast extract is a complex organic nitrogen source that provides amino acids, peptides, nucleotides, and vitamins, beneficial for microbial growth and riboflavin biosynthesis.
        - **Corn Steep Liquor:** Corn steep liquor is also used as a complex nitrogen source.
        - **Ammonium Salts:** Ammonium sulfate or ammonium phosphate can be used as supplementary inorganic nitrogen sources.
    - **Minerals:** Essential minerals are required for microbial growth and riboflavin biosynthesis.
        - **Phosphate:** Potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH₂PO₄) or ammonium phosphate.
        - **Magnesium:** Magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄).
        - **Trace Metals:** Iron (Fe), Zinc (Zn), Manganese (Mn), Molybdenum (Mo). Trace metal concentrations are important for enzyme activity and fermentation performance.
    - **Precursors (Optional, for some processes):** Riboflavin biosynthesis precursors, such as ribose or guanosine, may be added to enhance riboflavin production in some processes, although less common in industrial *Bacillus subtilis* fermentation.
- **Fermentation Conditions:**
    - **Fermentation Type:** **Fed-batch fermentation** is the commonly used mode for industrial riboflavin production. Fed-batch operation allows for controlled feeding of carbon source and other nutrients, avoiding catabolite repression, maintaining optimal substrate concentrations, and achieving high riboflavin titers.
    - **Aeration:** **Aerobic fermentation**. Riboflavin biosynthesis is an aerobic process, requiring sufficient dissolved oxygen for microbial growth and enzyme synthesis. High aeration rates and efficient oxygen transfer are crucial.
    - **Temperature:** Temperature is controlled within the optimal range for the specific microorganism, typically 30-35°C for *Bacillus subtilis* and slightly lower (25-30°C) for *Eremothecium ashbyii*.
    - **pH:** pH is controlled within a slightly acidic to neutral range, typically around pH 6.0-7.0. pH control is important for microbial growth and riboflavin biosynthesis.
    - **Agitation:** Agitation is used to ensure proper mixing, oxygen transfer, and temperature uniformity. Agitation intensity is optimized to balance mixing and aeration with shear sensitivity of *Bacillus subtilis* or fungal mycelia.
    - **Iron Limitation (for *Bacillus subtilis*):** Iron limitation is a key environmental factor that promotes riboflavin overproduction in *Bacillus subtilis*. Low iron concentrations reduce the activity of enzymes that divert precursors away from riboflavin biosynthesis, channeling metabolic flux towards riboflavin overproduction. Iron chelators may be added to further reduce bioavailable iron.
    - **Purine Nucleotide Limitation (for *Bacillus subtilis*):** Limitation of purine nucleotides (guanine, adenine) can also enhance riboflavin overproduction in *Bacillus subtilis*. Purine limitation reduces the demand for riboflavin precursors in nucleotide biosynthesis, making more precursors available for riboflavin overproduction.
- **Process Parameters and Control:** Similar to penicillin and protease fermentation, key parameters include DO, temperature, pH, nutrient feeding rate (for fed-batch), foam control, and process monitoring using SCADA systems.

**3.4.2.3 Product Recovery and Applications of Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin)**

**Product Recovery (Downstream Processing) of Riboflavin:**

Riboflavin recovery from fermentation broth depends on the producing microorganism and the form of riboflavin produced (intracellular, extracellular, or both). *Bacillus subtilis* typically produces riboflavin extracellularly, while *Eremothecium ashbyii* accumulates riboflavin intracellularly within mycelia.

1. **Cell Mass Removal (for *Bacillus subtilis* Fermentation - If necessary):** For *Bacillus subtilis* fermentation, cell mass removal may be less critical if riboflavin is primarily extracellular. However, clarification may be needed to remove residual cells and debris. Centrifugation or filtration can be used. For *Eremothecium ashbyii* fermentation, cell mass removal (mycelia removal) is necessary as riboflavin is intracellular.
2. **Riboflavin Extraction from Cell-Free Broth (for Extracellular Riboflavin) or Cell Lysate (for Intracellular Riboflavin):**
    - **For Extracellular Riboflavin (*Bacillus subtilis*):** Riboflavin is recovered from the clarified fermentation broth.
        - **Adsorption Chromatography:** Activated carbon adsorption is a common method for recovering riboflavin from fermentation broth. Riboflavin is adsorbed onto activated carbon, and then eluted using an alkaline or organic solvent eluent.
        - **Ion-Exchange Chromatography:** Ion-exchange chromatography can also be used to capture riboflavin from the broth based on its ionic properties.
    - **For Intracellular Riboflavin (*Eremothecium ashbyii*):** Cell disruption is necessary to release intracellular riboflavin from fungal mycelia.
        - **Cell Disruption Methods:** Physical methods (bead milling, homogenization) or chemical methods (alkali lysis) can be used to disrupt *Eremothecium ashbyii* mycelia.
        - **Solvent Extraction from Cell Lysate:** After cell disruption, riboflavin is extracted from the cell lysate using solvent extraction with organic solvents or aqueous extraction at alkaline pH.
3. **Purification (Chromatography):** Further purification of riboflavin extract is achieved using chromatography techniques.
    - **Adsorption Chromatography:** Adsorption chromatography can be used for further purification and decolorization of riboflavin extracts.
    - **Ion-Exchange Chromatography (IEX):** IEX can be used to remove ionic impurities and to fractionate riboflavin based on charge properties.
    - **Crystallization:** Crystallization is often used as a final purification step to obtain high-purity crystalline riboflavin.
4. **Crystallization of Riboflavin:** Riboflavin is crystallized from the purified solution to obtain a solid, crystalline riboflavin product.
    - **Crystallization Techniques:** Riboflavin crystallization is typically achieved by adjusting solvent composition, temperature, and pH to reduce riboflavin solubility and induce crystallization. Riboflavin has limited solubility in water, and crystallization from aqueous or aqueous-organic solvent mixtures is common.
    - **Drying:** Riboflavin crystals are separated by filtration or centrifugation and dried to obtain the final crystalline riboflavin product.

**Applications of Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin):**

Vitamin B2 (riboflavin) is an essential vitamin with important roles in human and animal nutrition. Major applications include:

- **Nutritional Supplement and Food Fortification:** The primary application of riboflavin.
    - **Dietary Supplement:** Riboflavin is used as a dietary supplement to prevent or treat riboflavin deficiency (ariboflavinosis).
    - **Food Fortification:** Riboflavin is used to fortify foods, such as breakfast cereals, flour, milk, and infant formulas, to increase riboflavin intake in the population and prevent deficiency.
- **Pharmaceutical Applications:**
    - **Treatment of Riboflavin Deficiency:** Riboflavin supplements are used to treat riboflavin deficiency and associated conditions.
    - **Component of Multivitamin Preparations:** Riboflavin is a common component of multivitamin and B-complex vitamin formulations.
    - **Phototherapy for Neonatal Jaundice:** Riboflavin is sometimes used as an adjunct to phototherapy for neonatal jaundice. Riboflavin can enhance the effectiveness of phototherapy in breaking down bilirubin.
- **Animal Feed Additive:** Riboflavin is added to animal feed, particularly for poultry, swine, and aquaculture, to ensure adequate riboflavin intake for animal growth and health.
- **Food Colorant (Natural Yellow Colorant):** Riboflavin has a bright yellow color and can be used as a natural food colorant (E101). However, its use as a colorant is limited due to its vitamin activity and regulatory considerations.
- **Research and Diagnostics:** Riboflavin and its fluorescent properties are used in biochemical research, fluorescence microscopy, and diagnostic assays. FAD and FMN coenzymes, derived from riboflavin, are essential cofactors in numerous enzymatic reactions studied in biochemistry and molecular biology.
