## **1.1 Domestic (Municipal) and Industrial Waste Water Treatments**

Wastewater treatment is a crucial process for environmental protection and public health. It involves removing contaminants from wastewater to make it safe for discharge back into the environment or for reuse.  Wastewater originates from various sources, broadly categorized as domestic (municipal) and industrial.

**Domestic (Municipal) Wastewater:** This type of wastewater is generated from residential, commercial, and institutional sources within a community. It primarily consists of human waste, household cleaning products, food scraps, and other materials that enter sewage systems. Municipal wastewater composition is relatively consistent but can vary based on local activities and seasonal changes.

**Industrial Wastewater:**  Industrial wastewater is produced by manufacturing and industrial processes. Its composition is highly variable and depends on the specific industry. Industrial wastewater can contain a wide array of pollutants, including heavy metals, organic chemicals, oils, grease, and process-specific contaminants.  Pre-treatment of industrial wastewater is often required before it can be discharged into municipal sewage systems or directly into the environment.

Wastewater treatment typically involves a sequence of stages, often categorized into primary, secondary, and tertiary treatments.

### **1.1.1 Primary Treatment**

Primary treatment is the first stage of wastewater treatment, focusing on the physical removal of large solids and suspended materials. It is a preliminary step designed to protect subsequent treatment processes and improve overall treatment efficiency.

![Fig. Primary Treatment Process](Unit I 1a53a995df8780f69720e68a76b33240/image%201.png)

Fig. Primary Treatment Process

**1.1.1.1 Screening and Grit Removal**

**Screening:**

- **Principle:** Screening is the first unit operation in most wastewater treatment plants. It involves passing wastewater through screens to remove large floating and suspended objects like rags, sticks, paper, plastics, and debris. These materials, if not removed, can cause operational problems in downstream processes, such as clogging pumps and pipes, and interfering with biological treatment.
- **Process Design:** Screens are typically made of parallel bars or mesh with openings of varying sizes. They can be classified as coarse or fine screens:
    - **Coarse Screens:** Have larger openings (typically > 6 mm) and are used to remove larger debris. They are often manually cleaned or mechanically raked.
    - **Fine Screens:** Have smaller openings (typically 1.5 to 6 mm) and are more effective at removing smaller solids. They are usually mechanically cleaned and require more frequent maintenance.
    - **Operational Parameters and Control:**
        - **Screen Size:** Selected based on the characteristics of the wastewater and the requirements of downstream processes.
        - **Cleaning Frequency:** Regular cleaning is essential to prevent clogging and maintain efficiency. Mechanically cleaned screens are often controlled by timers or headloss sensors.
        - **Disposal of Screenings:** Removed screenings are typically disposed of in landfills or incinerated after dewatering and compaction.
            
            ![Fig. Primary treatment process (a) screening process (b) grit chamber and (c) wastewater skimming tank.](Unit I 1a53a995df8780f69720e68a76b33240/image%202.png)
            
            Fig. Primary treatment process (a) screening process (b) grit chamber and (c) wastewater skimming tank.
            
    
    **Grit Removal:**
    
    - **Principle:** Grit removal is the process of separating heavier inorganic solids like sand, gravel, cinders, and eggshells from wastewater. Grit can cause abrasion and wear on pumps and other mechanical equipment, as well as accumulate in sedimentation basins and digesters, reducing their capacity.
    - **Process Design:** Grit removal is achieved through gravity separation based on density differences. Common grit removal systems include:
        - **Grit Channels:** Long, narrow channels designed to reduce the flow velocity to allow grit to settle while keeping organic solids in suspension. These were historically common but are less prevalent now due to space requirements and odor issues.
        - **Aerated Grit Chambers:** More compact and efficient than grit channels. Air is introduced into the chamber to create a spiral flow pattern. This promotes the settling of heavier grit particles while keeping lighter organic solids in suspension.
        - **Vortex Grit Separators:** Utilize centrifugal force to separate grit. Wastewater is introduced tangentially into a cylindrical chamber, creating a vortex. The heavier grit particles are forced to the outer wall and settle, while the lighter organic solids are carried out with the effluent.
- **Operational Parameters and Control:**
    - **Flow Velocity:** Carefully controlled to ensure efficient grit settling without excessive organic solids removal. For grit channels, a velocity of around 0.3 m/s (1 ft/s) is typically maintained.
    - **Aeration Rate (for aerated grit chambers):** Adjusted to optimize grit separation while minimizing the settling of organic matter.
    - **Grit Washing and Disposal:** Removed grit is often washed to remove organic matter before being disposed of in landfills.

**1.1.1.2 Sedimentation and Flotation**

**Sedimentation:**

- **Principle:** Sedimentation (or settling) is a gravity-based process used to remove suspended solids that are heavier than water. Wastewater is allowed to stand in a basin or tank for a sufficient period, allowing the solids to settle to the bottom due to gravity.
- **Process Design:** Sedimentation basins or tanks are designed to provide quiescent conditions that promote settling. Common types include:
    - **Rectangular Sedimentation Basins:** Simple to construct and operate. Wastewater flows horizontally through the basin, and settled solids are scraped to a sludge hopper at one end.
    - **Circular Sedimentation Basins:** Often equipped with rotating mechanisms that continuously scrape settled sludge towards a central hopper.
    - **Inclined Plate/Tube Settlers:** Enhance sedimentation efficiency by providing a larger settling area in a smaller footprint. Inclined plates or tubes are placed within the basin, reducing the vertical settling distance required for particles.
- **Operational Parameters and Control:**
    - **Detention Time:** The time wastewater is held in the sedimentation basin. Longer detention times generally lead to better solids removal but require larger basins. Typical detention times in primary sedimentation range from 1.5 to 2.5 hours.
    - **Surface Overflow Rate (SOR):** The flow rate of wastewater per unit surface area of the settling basin. Lower SOR values result in better settling.
    - **Solids Loading Rate:** The mass of solids applied per unit surface area per unit time. High loading rates can overload the system and reduce efficiency.
    - **Sludge Removal:** Regular removal of settled sludge is crucial to prevent anaerobic conditions and maintain basin capacity. Sludge is typically pumped to sludge processing facilities.

**Flotation:**

- **Principle:** Flotation is a process used to remove suspended solids and oil/grease that are lighter than water or have a tendency to float. Air bubbles are introduced into the wastewater, which attach to the particles, reducing their effective density and causing them to rise to the surface. The floated material is then skimmed off.
- **Process Design:** Different flotation methods exist, including:
    - **Dissolved Air Flotation (DAF):** The most common type. Air is dissolved in water under pressure and then released into the wastewater at atmospheric pressure. This creates fine air bubbles that effectively capture and float particles.
    - **Induced Air Flotation (IAF):** Air is drawn into the wastewater by mechanical means, such as impellers or turbines, creating larger air bubbles than DAF. IAF is less efficient for fine particles but can be suitable for oil and grease removal.
    - **Vacuum Flotation:** Pressure is reduced to create air bubbles. Less common than DAF or IAF.
- **Operational Parameters and Control:**
    - **Air-to-Solids Ratio (A/S):** The ratio of air mass to solids mass. Optimal A/S ratios are crucial for efficient flotation.
    - **Recycle Ratio (for DAF):** The proportion of treated effluent recycled and saturated with air. Higher recycle ratios can improve performance but increase energy consumption.
    - **Pressure (for DAF):** The pressure at which air is dissolved. Higher pressures lead to finer bubbles and better flotation.
    - **Chemical Addition:** Chemical coagulants and flocculants are often added to enhance particle aggregation and flotation efficiency, especially for fine particles and emulsified oils.

### **1.1.2 Secondary Treatment**

Secondary treatment follows primary treatment and focuses on removing biodegradable organic matter and suspended solids that remain after primary treatment. This stage primarily utilizes biological processes.

![Fig. Secondary Treatment Process](Unit I 1a53a995df8780f69720e68a76b33240/image%203.png)

Fig. Secondary Treatment Process

**1.1.2.1 Aerobic Biological Processes**

**Principle:** Aerobic biological processes rely on microorganisms that use oxygen to consume organic pollutants in wastewater. These microorganisms, primarily bacteria, oxidize organic matter into carbon dioxide, water, and new biomass.  Aerobic treatment is highly effective in reducing Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) and Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD), key indicators of organic pollution.

**Common Aerobic Biological Processes:**

- **Activated Sludge Process (ASP):** The most widely used aerobic treatment method.
    - **Process Description:** Wastewater is mixed with a microbial culture (activated sludge) in an aeration basin. Air or pure oxygen is supplied to maintain aerobic conditions, promoting the growth of microorganisms that consume organic pollutants. The mixture then flows to a secondary sedimentation tank where the microbial biomass (activated sludge) is separated from the treated effluent. A portion of the settled sludge is recycled back to the aeration basin to maintain a high concentration of microorganisms, while the excess sludge is removed for further processing.
    - **Variations of ASP:** Conventional ASP, extended aeration, sequencing batch reactors (SBRs), oxidation ditches, and membrane bioreactors (MBRs) are variations designed to optimize performance, reduce footprint, or address specific wastewater characteristics.
- **Trickling Filters (TFs):** One of the older but still relevant aerobic treatment technologies.
    - **Process Description:** Wastewater is sprayed or trickled over a bed of media (e.g., rocks, plastic media). A biofilm of microorganisms grows on the media surface. As wastewater flows over the biofilm, the microorganisms consume organic pollutants. Air circulates through the media bed, providing oxygen for the aerobic process. Treated effluent is collected at the bottom of the filter.
    - **Types of TFs:** Low-rate, high-rate, and roughing filters are classified based on hydraulic loading rates and media type.
- **Rotating Biological Contactors (RBCs):** Utilize rotating discs partially submerged in wastewater to develop a biofilm.
    - **Process Description:** Discs made of plastic or other materials are mounted on a rotating shaft and partially submerged in wastewater. As the discs rotate, they alternately expose the biofilm to wastewater and air. This allows microorganisms in the biofilm to absorb organic pollutants from the wastewater and then utilize oxygen from the air for their metabolism.
- **Aerated Lagoons:** Large, shallow basins where wastewater is treated biologically with the aid of artificial aeration.
    - **Process Description:** Aerators (surface aerators or diffused air systems) are used to supply oxygen to the lagoon. Naturally occurring or seeded microorganisms consume organic pollutants. Aerated lagoons are simpler and less energy-intensive than ASP but require larger land areas and may have lower treatment efficiency.

**Operational Parameters and Control for Aerobic Processes:**

- **Dissolved Oxygen (DO):** Maintaining sufficient DO levels (typically 2-4 mg/L) is critical for aerobic microbial activity. DO is monitored and controlled through aeration adjustments.
- **Nutrient Balance:** Microorganisms require nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus for growth. Nutrient deficiencies can limit treatment efficiency. Nutrient ratios (BOD:N:P) are monitored, and nutrients may be supplemented if needed.
- **pH:** Optimal pH range for most aerobic microorganisms is near neutral (6.5-7.5). pH is monitored and adjusted using chemicals like lime or acid if necessary.
- **Temperature:** Microbial activity is temperature-dependent. Mesophilic conditions (20-40°C) are generally optimal for wastewater treatment. Temperature fluctuations can affect treatment efficiency.
- **Sludge Retention Time (SRT) / Mean Cell Residence Time (MCRT):** The average time microorganisms are retained in the system. SRT/MCRT is a key parameter in ASP, influencing biomass concentration and treatment efficiency.
- **Food-to-Microorganism Ratio (F/M):** The ratio of organic substrate (food) to microbial biomass. F/M ratio affects microbial growth rate and sludge production.
- **Hydraulic Retention Time (HRT):** The average time wastewater is retained in the treatment unit. HRT influences the extent of treatment achieved.

**1.1.2.2 Anaerobic Biological Processes**

**Principle:** Anaerobic biological processes occur in the absence of oxygen.  In anaerobic digestion, a consortium of microorganisms breaks down organic matter through a series of biochemical reactions, ultimately producing biogas (primarily methane and carbon dioxide) and a stabilized sludge. Anaerobic treatment is particularly suitable for concentrated organic wastes and sludge stabilization.

**Key Stages in Anaerobic Digestion:**

1. **Hydrolysis:** Complex organic polymers (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids) are broken down into simpler soluble monomers (sugars, amino acids, fatty acids) by hydrolytic bacteria.
2. **Acidogenesis (Fermentation):** Acidogenic bacteria convert the soluble monomers into volatile fatty acids (VFAs), alcohols, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide.
3. **Acetogenesis:** Acetogenic bacteria convert VFAs (except acetate) and alcohols into acetate, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide.
4. **Methanogenesis:** Methanogenic archaea, a specialized group of microorganisms, convert acetate, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide into methane and carbon dioxide. This is the final and crucial step in anaerobic digestion, producing biogas.

**Common Anaerobic Biological Processes:**

- **Anaerobic Digesters:** Used for sludge stabilization and treatment of concentrated organic wastes.
    - **Types of Digesters:** Standard-rate digesters, high-rate digesters (completely mixed, contact process), and advanced digesters (thermophilic, two-phase).
    - **Process Design:** Digesters are typically closed reactors designed to maintain anaerobic conditions and optimal temperature. They can be batch or continuous flow.
    - **Biogas Production:** Biogas produced during anaerobic digestion can be used as a renewable energy source for heat and power generation.
- **Upflow Anaerobic Sludge Blanket (UASB) Reactors:** High-rate anaerobic reactors particularly effective for treating industrial wastewater with soluble organic pollutants.
    - **Process Description:** Wastewater is introduced at the bottom of the reactor and flows upward through a dense, granular sludge bed. The sludge bed consists of highly active anaerobic microorganisms that form granules. As wastewater passes through the sludge bed, organic pollutants are removed and converted to biogas. The biogas is collected at the top, and treated effluent is discharged.
- **Anaerobic Membrane Bioreactors (AnMBRs):** Combine anaerobic digestion with membrane filtration for enhanced solids retention and effluent quality.
    - **Process Description:** AnMBRs integrate a membrane filtration unit (microfiltration or ultrafiltration) directly with an anaerobic reactor. This allows for complete retention of biomass within the reactor, leading to high solids concentrations and efficient treatment. The membrane unit produces a high-quality effluent free of suspended solids.

**Operational Parameters and Control for Anaerobic Processes:**

- **Temperature:** Anaerobic digestion can occur under mesophilic (30-40°C) or thermophilic (50-60°C) conditions. Thermophilic digestion is faster but more energy-intensive and less stable. Mesophilic digestion is more common for wastewater treatment.
- **pH:** Optimal pH range for methanogenesis is near neutral (6.5-7.5). pH control is important to prevent acidification and process inhibition.
- **Volatile Fatty Acids (VFAs):** VFA concentration is an indicator of process stability. Accumulation of VFAs can indicate imbalance and potential process failure.
- **Alkalinity:** Alkalinity provides buffering capacity to neutralize VFAs and maintain pH stability.
- **Hydraulic Retention Time (HRT):** HRT in anaerobic reactors is typically longer than in aerobic systems, ranging from days to weeks, depending on the process and waste characteristics.
- **Organic Loading Rate (OLR):** The amount of organic matter fed to the reactor per unit volume per unit time. OLR needs to be carefully controlled to avoid overloading and process instability.
- **Nutrient Balance:** While nutrient requirements are lower than in aerobic processes, trace nutrients are still necessary for microbial growth and activity.
- **Biogas Production and Composition:** Biogas production rate and methane content are important indicators of process performance.

### **1.1.3 Tertiary Treatment (Advanced Treatment)**

Tertiary treatment, also known as advanced wastewater treatment, is the final stage of treatment, aimed at removing specific pollutants that are not effectively removed by primary and secondary processes. Tertiary treatment is often required to meet stringent water quality standards for reuse or sensitive receiving environments.

**1.1.3.1 Advanced Oxidation Processes (AOPs)**

**Principle:** AOPs are a set of chemical treatment procedures used to remove organic materials in wastewater by oxidation through reactions with hydroxyl radicals (•OH). Hydroxyl radicals are very reactive and non-selective oxidants that can degrade a wide range of organic pollutants, including recalcitrant and toxic compounds that are resistant to conventional biological treatment.

**Common AOPs used in Wastewater Treatment:**

- **Ozonation (O₃):** Ozone is a powerful oxidant that can directly react with organic compounds or decompose to form hydroxyl radicals. Ozonation is effective for disinfection, color removal, and degradation of some organic pollutants.
- **UV/Hydrogen Peroxide (UV/H₂O₂):** Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) is activated by UV radiation to generate hydroxyl radicals. This combination is effective for removing a wide range of organic pollutants, including pharmaceuticals and endocrine-disrupting compounds.
- **Fenton Process (Fe²⁺/H₂O₂):** Ferrous ions (Fe²⁺) catalyze the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) to produce hydroxyl radicals. The Fenton process is effective for treating industrial wastewater containing recalcitrant organic pollutants, but it operates best at acidic pH.
- **Photo-Fenton (UV/Fe²⁺/H₂O₂):** Combines the Fenton process with UV irradiation to enhance hydroxyl radical generation and improve treatment efficiency.
- **Titanium Dioxide Photocatalysis (TiO₂/UV):** Titanium dioxide (TiO₂) is a semiconductor photocatalyst that, when irradiated with UV light, generates electron-hole pairs. These pairs can react with water and oxygen to produce hydroxyl radicals and other reactive oxygen species, which can degrade organic pollutants.

**Operational Parameters and Control for AOPs:**

- **Oxidant Dosage (O₃, H₂O₂):** Optimal dosage is crucial for effective pollutant removal without excessive oxidant consumption or by-product formation.
- **UV Intensity (for UV-based AOPs):** Sufficient UV intensity is necessary to activate oxidants or photocatalysts.
- **pH:** pH can significantly affect the efficiency of AOPs. For example, the Fenton process is most effective at acidic pH, while ozonation is less pH-dependent.
- **Reaction Time:** Sufficient reaction time is needed for the oxidation reactions to proceed to the desired level.
- **Catalyst Concentration (for Fenton and photocatalysis):** Optimal catalyst concentration is required to maximize hydroxyl radical generation without causing adverse effects.
- **Turbidity and Suspended Solids:** High turbidity and suspended solids can interfere with UV penetration and reduce the efficiency of UV-based AOPs. Pre-treatment to remove solids may be necessary.

**1.1.3.2 Membrane Filtration**

**Principle:** Membrane filtration processes use semi-permeable membranes to physically separate pollutants from water based on size exclusion. Pressure is applied to force water through the membrane, while pollutants are retained. Membrane filtration can remove a wide range of contaminants, including suspended solids, bacteria, viruses, and dissolved substances, depending on the membrane pore size.

**Common Membrane Filtration Processes in Wastewater Treatment:**

- **Microfiltration (MF):** Removes suspended solids, bacteria, and protozoa. Pore size range: 0.1-10 µm.
- **Ultrafiltration (UF):** Removes viruses, colloids, and larger organic molecules, in addition to particles removed by MF. Pore size range: 0.01-0.1 µm. MF and UF are often used as pre-treatment for reverse osmosis or as standalone tertiary treatment for particulate removal.
- **Nanofiltration (NF):** Removes multivalent ions (e.g., hardness, nitrates, sulfates), some dissolved organic matter, and viruses. Pore size range: 0.001-0.01 µm. NF is used for softening, color removal, and removal of specific dissolved pollutants.
- **Reverse Osmosis (RO):** The most advanced membrane filtration process. Removes virtually all dissolved salts, minerals, organic compounds, bacteria, and viruses. Pore size range: < 0.001 µm. RO is used for water reuse, desalination, and producing high-purity water.

**Process Design and Operational Parameters for Membrane Filtration:**

- **Membrane Type and Material:** Membranes are made of various materials (polymeric, ceramic) and configurations (plate-and-frame, spiral-wound, hollow fiber). Membrane selection depends on the wastewater characteristics and treatment goals.
- **Transmembrane Pressure (TMP):** The pressure difference across the membrane. Higher TMP increases flux (flow rate through the membrane) but also increases fouling potential.
- **Flux:** The flow rate of permeate (treated water) per unit membrane area. Flux is affected by TMP, membrane fouling, and wastewater characteristics.
- **Recovery Rate:** The percentage of feed water that is recovered as permeate. Higher recovery rates reduce water waste but increase concentrate volume and fouling potential.
- **Fouling Control:** Membrane fouling (accumulation of materials on the membrane surface) is a major challenge. Fouling control strategies include:
    - **Pre-treatment:** Removing solids and other foulants before membrane filtration.
    - **Backwashing:** Reversing the flow through the membrane to dislodge accumulated foulants.
    - **Chemical Cleaning:** Using chemical agents to remove stubborn fouling layers.
    - **Air Scouring:** Introducing air bubbles to the membrane surface to reduce fouling.

**1.1.3.3 Disinfection**

**Principle:** Disinfection is the final step in wastewater treatment, aimed at inactivating or killing pathogenic microorganisms (bacteria, viruses, protozoa) to ensure the treated effluent is safe for discharge or reuse. Disinfection is crucial for protecting public health and preventing waterborne diseases.

**Common Disinfection Methods in Wastewater Treatment:**

- **Chlorination:** The most widely used disinfection method globally. Chlorine (in the form of chlorine gas, hypochlorite, or chloramines) is a strong oxidant that effectively inactivates microorganisms.
    - **Mechanism:** Chlorine disrupts microbial cell membranes and interferes with essential cellular processes.
    - **Advantages:** Effective, relatively inexpensive, and provides residual disinfection.
    - **Disadvantages:** Formation of disinfection by-products (DBPs) like trihalomethanes (THMs) and haloacetic acids (HAAs), which are regulated due to health concerns. Dechlorination may be required before discharge to protect aquatic life from chlorine toxicity.
- **Ultraviolet (UV) Disinfection:** Uses UV radiation to inactivate microorganisms.
    - **Mechanism:** UV radiation damages the DNA and RNA of microorganisms, preventing them from replicating.
    - **Advantages:** Effective against a wide range of pathogens, no DBPs are formed, and no residual toxicity.
    - **Disadvantages:** No residual disinfection, effectiveness can be reduced by turbidity and suspended solids, and higher energy consumption compared to chlorination.
- **Ozonation (O₃):** As mentioned earlier, ozone is a powerful oxidant that can be used for both oxidation of organic pollutants and disinfection.
    - **Mechanism:** Ozone damages microbial cell walls and reacts with cellular components.
    - **Advantages:** Effective disinfection, strong oxidant, and decomposes to oxygen, minimizing residual concerns.
    - **Disadvantages:** Relatively expensive, no residual disinfection, and potential formation of bromate in bromide-containing waters.
- **Chlorine Dioxide (ClO₂):** Another chlorine-based disinfectant, but it produces fewer DBPs than chlorination.
    - **Mechanism:** Chlorine dioxide oxidizes microbial cell components.
    - **Advantages:** Effective disinfection, fewer DBPs than chlorination, and effective over a wider pH range.
    - **Disadvantages:** More expensive than chlorination, and potential formation of chlorite and chlorate, which are regulated.
- **Peracetic Acid (PAA):** An emerging disinfectant, particularly for wastewater reuse applications.
    - **Mechanism:** PAA is a strong oxidant that disrupts microbial cell membranes and oxidizes cellular components.
    - **Advantages:** Effective disinfection, biodegradable, and forms fewer DBPs compared to chlorination.
    - **Disadvantages:** Relatively more expensive than chlorination and less widely used.

**Operational Parameters and Control for Disinfection:**

- **Disinfectant Dosage:** Optimal dosage is crucial for effective disinfection while minimizing chemical costs and potential by-product formation. Dosage is determined based on wastewater characteristics, target pathogen inactivation levels, and regulations.
- **Contact Time:** Sufficient contact time is needed for the disinfectant to effectively inactivate microorganisms. Contact time depends on the disinfectant type, dosage, and wastewater characteristics.
- **Turbidity and Suspended Solids:** Turbidity and suspended solids can shield microorganisms from disinfectants, reducing disinfection efficiency. Pre-treatment to remove solids is often necessary, especially for UV disinfection.
- **pH and Temperature:** pH and temperature can influence the effectiveness of some disinfectants, particularly chlorination.
- **Residual Disinfectant (for Chlorination):** Maintaining a residual chlorine concentration in the treated effluent provides ongoing disinfection and protects against microbial regrowth in distribution systems. However, dechlorination may be required before discharge to receiving waters.

## **1.2 Important Microorganisms in Waste Water Treatment**

Microorganisms are the workhorses of biological wastewater treatment processes. They are responsible for the breakdown and removal of pollutants. Different types of microorganisms thrive in aerobic and anaerobic conditions and play specific roles in wastewater treatment.

### **1.2.1 Aerobic Microorganisms**

Aerobic microorganisms require oxygen to carry out their metabolic processes. In wastewater treatment, they are primarily responsible for the oxidation of organic pollutants.

**1.2.1.1 Bacteria (Pseudomonas, Nitrosomonas, etc.)**

- **Pseudomonas:** A diverse genus of Gram-negative bacteria, ubiquitous in soil and water environments. Many *Pseudomonas* species are versatile in their metabolic capabilities and can degrade a wide range of organic compounds, including hydrocarbons, pesticides, and industrial pollutants. In activated sludge and other aerobic treatment systems, *Pseudomonas* species contribute significantly to the removal of diverse organic substrates.
- **Nitrosomonas:** A genus of Gram-negative, chemolithoautotrophic bacteria known for their role in nitrification, a crucial step in nitrogen removal in wastewater treatment. *Nitrosomonas* bacteria oxidize ammonia (NH₃) to nitrite (NO₂⁻). This is the first step of nitrification, converting ammonia, a toxic form of nitrogen, into a less toxic form.
- **Other Important Aerobic Bacteria:**
    - **Nitrobacter:** Another genus of Gram-negative, chemolithoautotrophic bacteria involved in nitrification. *Nitrobacter* bacteria oxidize nitrite (NO₂⁻) to nitrate (NO₃⁻), completing the nitrification process.
    - **Acinetobacter:** Gram-negative bacteria known for their ability to accumulate phosphorus. They are important in enhanced biological phosphorus removal (EBPR) processes in wastewater treatment.
    - **Zoogloea:** Gram-negative bacteria that form flocs (aggregates) in activated sludge. Floc-forming bacteria are essential for settling and biomass separation in ASP.
    - **Bacillus:** A genus of Gram-positive, spore-forming bacteria. Some *Bacillus* species are involved in the degradation of complex organic compounds and can contribute to various stages of wastewater treatment.

**1.2.1.2 Fungi (Trichoderma, Aspergillus, etc.)**

- **Trichoderma:** A genus of filamentous fungi widely distributed in soil and decaying wood. *Trichoderma* species are known for their ability to degrade cellulose and other complex carbohydrates. In wastewater treatment, they can contribute to the breakdown of plant-based materials and other cellulosic pollutants. Some *Trichoderma* species also exhibit bioremediation capabilities for certain industrial pollutants.
- **Aspergillus:** Another genus of filamentous fungi, also common in soil and decaying organic matter. *Aspergillus* species are versatile in their metabolic capabilities and can degrade a wide range of organic substrates, including complex carbohydrates, proteins, and some recalcitrant compounds. They can play a role in the treatment of industrial wastewater containing diverse organic pollutants.
- **Other Important Aerobic Fungi:**
    - **Penicillium:** A genus of filamentous fungi known for producing penicillin antibiotics. Some *Penicillium* species can also degrade organic pollutants and contribute to wastewater treatment.
    - **Geotrichum:** A genus of yeast-like fungi that can degrade various organic compounds, including hydrocarbons and phenolic compounds. They can be found in activated sludge and other aerobic treatment systems.

### **1.2.2 Anaerobic Microorganisms**

Anaerobic microorganisms thrive in the absence of oxygen and are essential for anaerobic digestion processes. They are responsible for the complex biochemical reactions that break down organic matter and produce biogas.

**1.2.2.1 Bacteria (Methanogenic archaea, Syntrophic bacteria, etc.)**

- **Methanogenic Archaea:** A distinct group of microorganisms belonging to the domain Archaea. Methanogens are strictly anaerobic and are responsible for the final step of anaerobic digestion – methanogenesis. They convert acetate, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide into methane (CH₄) and carbon dioxide (CO₂), the primary components of biogas.
    - **Types of Methanogens:** Acetoclastic methanogens (e.g., *Methanosarcina*, *Methanosaeta*) convert acetate directly to methane and carbon dioxide. Hydrogenotrophic methanogens (e.g., *Methanobacterium*, *Methanococcus*) use hydrogen and carbon dioxide to produce methane.
- **Syntrophic Bacteria:** A group of bacteria that play a crucial role in anaerobic digestion by facilitating the degradation of volatile fatty acids (VFAs) other than acetate. Syntrophic bacteria cannot directly utilize VFAs like propionate and butyrate under anaerobic conditions. They must cooperate with hydrogenotrophic methanogens in a syntrophic relationship.
    - **Syntrophic Acetate Oxidation Bacteria (SAOB):** Oxidize acetate to carbon dioxide and hydrogen under certain conditions, especially when hydrogen partial pressure is low.
    - **Syntrophic VFA-Oxidizing Bacteria:** Oxidize VFAs like propionate and butyrate to acetate, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen. The hydrogen produced is then consumed by hydrogenotrophic methanogens, maintaining a low hydrogen partial pressure that is thermodynamically favorable for VFA oxidation.
- **Other Important Anaerobic Bacteria:**
    - **Hydrolytic Bacteria:** A diverse group of bacteria that perform hydrolysis, the first step of anaerobic digestion. They secrete extracellular enzymes (e.g., cellulases, proteases, lipases) to break down complex organic polymers into simpler monomers. Examples include *Clostridium*, *Bacteroides*, and *Streptococcus* species.
    - **Acidogenic Bacteria (Fermentative Bacteria):** Convert soluble monomers produced by hydrolysis into VFAs, alcohols, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide through fermentation. Examples include *Escherichia*, *Enterobacter*, and *Lactobacillus* species.
    - **Acetogenic Bacteria:** Convert VFAs (except acetate) and alcohols into acetate, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide. Examples include *Syntrophobacter* and *Syntrophomonas* species.

**1.2.2.2 Fungi (Saccharomyces, Pichia, etc.)**

- **Saccharomyces:** A genus of yeasts, commonly known as brewer's yeast or baker's yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*). While primarily known for alcoholic fermentation in food and beverage industries, some *Saccharomyces* species can also contribute to anaerobic digestion, particularly in the fermentation of sugars and other readily biodegradable substrates. However, their role in complex wastewater treatment is less significant compared to anaerobic bacteria and methanogens.
- **Pichia:** Another genus of yeasts. Some *Pichia* species have been explored for their potential in anaerobic digestion, particularly in the fermentation of specific substrates. However, their role in conventional anaerobic wastewater treatment is also less prominent compared to bacteria and archaea.
- **Anaerobic Fungi (Neocallimastigomycota):** A specialized group of fungi found in the rumen of herbivores and anaerobic environments. These fungi are highly effective in degrading plant biomass, including cellulose and hemicellulose, under anaerobic conditions. They produce a range of enzymes that can break down complex plant cell walls. Anaerobic fungi are gaining attention for their potential application in anaerobic digestion of lignocellulosic biomass in wastewater treatment.

### **1.2.3 Principles of Growth and Plasmid-Borne Metabolic Activities**

**Principles of Microbial Growth:**

- **Microbial growth** refers to the increase in the number of microbial cells in a population, not the increase in size of individual cells.
- **Growth Curve:** In a batch culture, microbial growth typically follows a characteristic growth curve with four phases:
    1. **Lag Phase:** Period of adaptation where microorganisms adjust to the new environment. Little or no growth occurs.
    2. **Exponential (Log) Phase:** Period of rapid and constant growth. Microorganisms are dividing at their maximum rate. Growth rate is influenced by nutrient availability, temperature, pH, and other environmental factors.
    3. **Stationary Phase:** Growth rate slows down and eventually becomes zero. Nutrient depletion, accumulation of waste products, and other limiting factors cause growth to cease. The number of viable cells remains constant.
    4. **Death Phase:** Number of viable cells declines due to cell death.
- **Factors Affecting Microbial Growth:**
    - **Nutrients:** Carbon source, nitrogen source, phosphorus, sulfur, essential minerals, and growth factors are required for microbial growth.
    - **Temperature:** Microorganisms have optimal, minimum, and maximum growth temperatures. Wastewater treatment processes are typically operated in mesophilic (20-40°C) or thermophilic (50-60°C) ranges.
    - **pH:** Optimal pH range for most wastewater treatment microorganisms is near neutral (6.5-7.5).
    - **Oxygen (for aerobic microorganisms):** Aerobic microorganisms require oxygen as the terminal electron acceptor in respiration.
    - **Water Availability:** Water is essential for microbial metabolism and transport of nutrients and waste products.
    - **Osmotic Pressure:** High or low osmotic pressure can inhibit microbial growth.
    - **Salinity:** High salinity can be inhibitory to many microorganisms.
    - **Toxic Substances:** Heavy metals, organic pollutants, and other toxic substances can inhibit or kill microorganisms.

**Plasmid-Borne Metabolic Activities:**

- **Plasmids:** Extrachromosomal, self-replicating DNA molecules found in bacteria, archaea, and some fungi. Plasmids are not essential for cell survival under normal conditions but can carry genes that confer beneficial traits, including metabolic activities relevant to wastewater treatment.
- **Plasmid-Borne Genes in Wastewater Treatment:**
    - **Antibiotic Resistance Genes:** Plasmids often carry genes that confer resistance to antibiotics. The spread of antibiotic resistance genes in wastewater treatment systems is a growing concern.
    - **Metal Resistance Genes:** Plasmids can carry genes that enable microorganisms to tolerate or detoxify heavy metals, which are common pollutants in industrial wastewater.
    - **Degradative Genes:** Plasmids can carry genes encoding enzymes that degrade specific organic pollutants, such as pesticides, herbicides, and industrial chemicals. For example, plasmids in *Pseudomonas* species can carry genes for the degradation of hydrocarbons and other recalcitrant compounds.
    - **Virulence Factors:** In pathogenic bacteria, plasmids can carry virulence genes that enhance pathogenicity.
- **Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT):** Plasmids can be transferred between microorganisms through HGT mechanisms, such as conjugation, transformation, and transduction. HGT contributes to the spread of plasmid-borne genes, including antibiotic resistance and degradative capabilities, within microbial communities in wastewater treatment systems.
- **Metabolic Activities Influenced by Plasmids:** Plasmid-borne genes can enhance the metabolic versatility and adaptability of microorganisms in wastewater treatment. They can enable microorganisms to degrade a wider range of pollutants, tolerate toxic substances, and adapt to changing environmental conditions. However, the spread of antibiotic resistance plasmids is a significant challenge that needs to be addressed in wastewater treatment and public health.

## **1.3 Aerobic Biological Treatments**

Aerobic biological treatments are central to secondary wastewater treatment, effectively removing biodegradable organic matter using microorganisms in oxygen-rich environments.

### **1.3.1 Activated Sludge Process (ASP)**

The Activated Sludge Process (ASP), developed in the early 20th century by **Gilbert J. Fowler, Edward Ardern, and William T. Lockett** in England around **1914**, is the most widely used aerobic secondary treatment method globally.

![Fig. Activated Sludge Process](Unit I 1a53a995df8780f69720e68a76b33240/image%206.png)

Fig. Activated Sludge Process

**1.3.1.1 Principles and Process Design**

**Principles of ASP:**

- **Biological Oxidation:** ASP relies on a complex community of microorganisms, primarily bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and rotifers, to biologically oxidize biodegradable organic pollutants in wastewater.
- **Floc Formation:** Microorganisms in ASP aggregate to form flocs (activated sludge). These flocs are crucial for settling and biomass separation in the secondary sedimentation tank. Floc formation is promoted by extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) produced by microorganisms.
- **Suspended Growth System:** ASP is a suspended growth system, meaning microorganisms are suspended in the wastewater and are not attached to a fixed medium.
- **Recirculation of Activated Sludge:** A key feature of ASP is the recirculation of settled activated sludge from the secondary sedimentation tank back to the aeration basin. This maintains a high concentration of microorganisms in the aeration basin, enhancing treatment efficiency and stability.
- **Aeration:** Oxygen is supplied to the aeration basin, typically through diffused air systems or surface aerators, to maintain aerobic conditions necessary for microbial respiration and organic matter oxidation.

**Process Design of ASP:**

A typical ASP system consists of the following components:

1. **Aeration Basin (Reactor):** The heart of the ASP system where biological oxidation of organic pollutants occurs.
    - **Types of Aeration Basins:**
        - **Conventional Plug Flow:** Wastewater flows in a plug-like manner through a long, narrow basin. Substrate concentration decreases along the basin length.
        - **Complete Mix (Continuously Stirred Tank Reactor - CSTR):** Wastewater is rapidly and completely mixed throughout the basin. Substrate concentration is uniform throughout the basin.
        - **Step Feed:** Wastewater is introduced at multiple points along the aeration basin length to distribute the organic load and improve treatment efficiency.
        - **Sequencing Batch Reactor (SBR):** A fill-and-draw system where all treatment steps (fill, react, settle, decant, idle) occur in the same reactor in a time sequence. SBRs are flexible and suitable for smaller plants or variable flow conditions.
        - **Oxidation Ditch:** A modified ASP configuration, typically a long, continuous loop reactor with extended aeration times. Oxidation ditches are effective for nitrification and denitrification.
    - **Aeration Systems:**
        - **Diffused Air Systems:** Compressed air is pumped through diffusers at the bottom of the basin, creating fine air bubbles that transfer oxygen to the wastewater.
        - **Surface Aerators:** Mechanical devices that agitate the water surface, promoting oxygen transfer from the atmosphere. Types include:
            - **Vertical Turbine Aerators:** Impellers mounted on a vertical shaft that mix and aerate the water.
            - **Horizontal Paddle Aerators:** Paddles mounted on a horizontal shaft that rotate and splash water into the air.
2. **Secondary Sedimentation Tank (Clarifier):** Used to separate the activated sludge biomass from the treated effluent.
    - **Design Considerations:** Similar to primary sedimentation tanks (rectangular or circular). Designed for quiescent settling conditions.
    - **Sludge Collection and Recirculation:** Settled sludge is collected and a portion is recycled back to the aeration basin (Return Activated Sludge - RAS). Excess sludge is removed as Waste Activated Sludge (WAS) for further processing.
3. **Return Activated Sludge (RAS) System:** Pumps and piping to recycle settled sludge from the secondary clarifier back to the aeration basin. RAS flow rate is a key operational parameter.
4. **Waste Activated Sludge (WAS) System:** System for removing excess sludge from the ASP system. WAS is typically sent to sludge thickening and digestion processes.

**Process Design Parameters for ASP:**

- **Hydraulic Retention Time (HRT):** Typically ranges from 4 to 8 hours for conventional ASP. Extended aeration systems have longer HRTs (18-36 hours).
- **Solids Retention Time (SRT) / Mean Cell Residence Time (MCRT):** Ranges from 5 to 15 days for conventional ASP and longer for extended aeration systems (20-30 days). SRT/MCRT is a critical design parameter that influences biomass concentration, sludge production, and nitrification potential.
- **Food-to-Microorganism Ratio (F/M):** Typically ranges from 0.2 to 0.4 kg BOD/kg MLVSS·d for conventional ASP. F/M ratio affects sludge production and effluent quality.
- **Mixed Liquor Suspended Solids (MLSS):** Concentration of microorganisms in the aeration basin. MLSS typically ranges from 2000 to 4000 mg/L for conventional ASP.
- **Mixed Liquor Volatile Suspended Solids (MLVSS):** The organic fraction of MLSS, representing the active microbial biomass. MLVSS/MLSS ratio is typically around 0.7-0.8.
- **Aeration Rate:** Determined based on oxygen demand of the wastewater and desired DO levels in the aeration basin.

**1.3.1.2 Operational Parameters and Control**

**Key Operational Parameters for ASP:**

- **Dissolved Oxygen (DO):** Maintain DO levels in the aeration basin between 2-4 mg/L for optimal aerobic treatment. Low DO can lead to anaerobic conditions and reduced treatment efficiency. High DO increases energy consumption. DO is typically monitored using DO probes and controlled by adjusting aeration rates.
- **Nutrient Balance (N:P Ratio):** Maintain a balanced nutrient ratio (BOD:N:P approximately 100:5:1) to ensure adequate nutrients for microbial growth. Nutrient deficiencies can limit treatment efficiency. Nutrient supplementation may be required for industrial wastewater deficient in nitrogen or phosphorus.
- **pH:** Maintain pH in the neutral range (6.5-7.5) for optimal microbial activity. pH is monitored and adjusted using chemicals (lime, acid) if necessary.
- **Temperature:** Maintain temperature within the mesophilic range (20-40°C) for optimal treatment. Temperature fluctuations can affect microbial activity and settling characteristics of sludge.
- **Sludge Volume Index (SVI):** A measure of sludge settling characteristics. SVI is the volume (in mL) occupied by 1 gram of activated sludge after settling for 30 minutes. Low SVI (<80 mL/g) indicates good settling sludge, while high SVI (>150 mL/g) indicates poor settling sludge (bulking sludge). SVI is monitored regularly to assess sludge settleability.
- **Sludge Age (SRT):** SRT is a crucial operational parameter for controlling the microbial population and treatment efficiency. Adjusting WAS rate controls SRT. Increasing SRT promotes nitrification and reduces sludge production but may require larger aeration basins.
- **Mean Cell Residence Time (MCRT):** Synonymous with SRT in many ASP systems.
- **Food-to-Microorganism Ratio (F/M):** F/M ratio is controlled by adjusting WAS rate or influent organic loading. Maintaining an optimal F/M ratio is important for preventing sludge bulking and ensuring efficient treatment.
- **Return Activated Sludge (RAS) Rate:** RAS rate is adjusted to maintain desired MLSS concentration in the aeration basin and to control sludge blanket level in the secondary clarifier.
- **Wastage Rate (WAS):** WAS rate is adjusted to control SRT/MCRT and F/M ratio.
- **Microscopic Examination of Activated Sludge:** Regular microscopic examination of activated sludge can provide valuable insights into the health and composition of the microbial community. Filamentous bacteria overgrowth can indicate sludge bulking problems. Presence of protozoa and rotifers indicates a healthy and well-functioning ASP system.

**Control Strategies for ASP:**

- **DO Control:** Automated DO control systems using DO probes and variable speed aerators or aeration control valves.
- **pH Control:** Automated pH control systems with pH probes and chemical dosing pumps.
- **Sludge Settling Monitoring:** Online SVI monitoring or sludge blanket level monitoring in the secondary clarifier.
- **Nutrient Monitoring and Dosing:** Online nutrient analyzers and automated nutrient dosing systems.
- **Process Monitoring and Control Software:** SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) systems for real-time monitoring and control of ASP parameters.
- **Troubleshooting and Problem Solving:** Operators need to be trained to troubleshoot common ASP problems like sludge bulking, foaming, and nitrification/denitrification issues.

### **1.3.2 Rotating Biological Contactors (RBCs)**

Rotating Biological Contactors (RBCs) were developed in Germany in the **early 1900s**, with significant advancements made in the **1950s and 1960s**.

![Fig. Rotating Biological Contractors (RBCs)](Unit I 1a53a995df8780f69720e68a76b33240/image%207.png)

Fig. Rotating Biological Contractors (RBCs)

**1.3.2.1 Principles and Process Design**

**Principles of RBCs:**

- **Biofilm-Based Process:** RBCs are biofilm-based aerobic treatment systems. Microorganisms grow as a biofilm on the surface of rotating discs.
- **Attached Growth System:** Unlike ASP, RBCs are attached growth systems, where microorganisms are attached to a fixed medium (rotating discs).
- **Alternating Wetting and Drying:** As the discs rotate, the biofilm is alternately submerged in wastewater and exposed to air. This allows microorganisms to absorb organic pollutants from the wastewater during submergence and then utilize oxygen from the air during exposure.
- **Oxygen Transfer:** Oxygen transfer occurs primarily during the atmospheric exposure phase as the biofilm is in contact with air.
- **Sloughing of Excess Biomass:** Excess biofilm growth sloughs off the discs due to shear forces and gravity. Sloughed biomass is carried with the treated effluent and is removed in a secondary clarifier.

**Process Design of RBCs:**

A typical RBC system consists of the following components:

1. **RBC Unit:** A series of closely spaced circular discs mounted on a horizontal shaft. Discs are typically made of plastic (e.g., high-density polyethylene or polystyrene).
    - **Stages:** RBC systems are often designed in multiple stages (e.g., 3-4 stages in series). Each stage consists of a set of discs in a tank. Wastewater flows sequentially through the stages. The first stage receives the highest organic load, and subsequent stages progressively reduce the remaining BOD.
    - **Submergence:** Typically, about 40% of the disc surface area is submerged in wastewater at any given time.
    - **Rotation Speed:** Discs rotate slowly, typically at 1-2 revolutions per minute (rpm). Rotation speed is optimized for biofilm growth, oxygen transfer, and sloughing of excess biomass.
    - **Media Surface Area:** The total surface area of the discs in an RBC unit is a key design parameter. Higher surface area provides more space for biofilm growth and enhances treatment capacity.
2. **Primary Clarification (Pre-treatment):** Primary treatment (screening and sedimentation) is essential before RBCs to remove large solids that could clog or damage the discs and reduce treatment efficiency.
3. **Secondary Clarification (Post-treatment):** A secondary sedimentation tank is needed after the RBC unit to remove sloughed biomass from the treated effluent.
4. **Drive System:** A motor and gear reducer system to rotate the shaft and discs.
5. **Cover (Optional):** RBC units are sometimes covered to reduce odors, control algae growth, and protect against weather conditions.

**Process Design Parameters for RBCs:**

- **Hydraulic Loading Rate (HLR):** The flow rate of wastewater per unit surface area of the RBC media. HLR typically ranges from 0.02 to 0.08 m³/m²·d (0.5 to 2 gpd/ft²). Lower HLRs generally result in better treatment but require larger RBC units.
- **Organic Loading Rate (OLR):** The mass of BOD applied per unit surface area of the RBC media per unit time. OLR typically ranges from 5 to 20 g BOD/m²·d (1 to 4 lb BOD/1000 ft²·d). OLR is a key design parameter that influences treatment efficiency.
- **Number of Stages:** Typically 3-4 stages are used in series to achieve desired BOD removal.
- **Disc Diameter and Spacing:** Discs typically range from 2 to 4 meters in diameter. Spacing between discs is designed to allow for biofilm growth and prevent clogging.
- **Rotation Speed:** Typically 1-2 rpm.
- **Media Type and Surface Area:** Plastic media (HDPE, polystyrene) with high surface area-to-volume ratio are commonly used.

**1.3.2.2 Operational Parameters and Control**

**Key Operational Parameters for RBCs:**

- **Rotation Speed:** Maintain rotation speed within the design range (1-2 rpm). Adjustments may be needed to optimize biofilm growth and sloughing.
- **Hydraulic Loading Rate (HLR):** Monitor influent flow rate and adjust as needed to maintain design HLR. Overloading can reduce treatment efficiency.
- **Organic Loading Rate (OLR):** Monitor influent BOD concentration and flow rate to calculate OLR. Adjustments to influent loading or RBC capacity may be needed if OLR exceeds design values.
- **Dissolved Oxygen (DO):** While RBCs are primarily aerobic, DO levels in the wastewater can be monitored. Adequate oxygen transfer is generally achieved through atmospheric exposure during rotation.
- **Biofilm Thickness and Condition:** Regular visual inspection of biofilm thickness and condition. Excessive biofilm growth can lead to clogging and reduced oxygen transfer. Sloughing of excess biomass is a normal process.
- **Temperature:** Temperature affects microbial activity. Performance may decrease at low temperatures.
- **pH:** Maintain pH in the neutral range (6.5-7.5) for optimal microbial activity.
- **Nutrient Balance:** Ensure adequate nutrients (N, P) are present for microbial growth.

**Control Strategies for RBCs:**

- **Rotation Speed Control:** Maintain constant rotation speed or adjust based on process monitoring.
- **Flow Control:** Flow equalization to maintain consistent hydraulic loading.
- **Biofilm Monitoring:** Regular visual inspection and microscopic examination of biofilm.
- **Sloughed Biomass Removal:** Efficient removal of sloughed biomass in the secondary clarifier is crucial to prevent solids carryover in the effluent.
- **Maintenance:** Regular maintenance of drive system, discs, and clarifier.
- **Troubleshooting and Problem Solving:** Operators need to be trained to troubleshoot common RBC problems like excessive biofilm growth, clogging, and reduced treatment efficiency.

### **1.4.1 Air Lift Membrane Bioreactors (AnMBRs)**

Air Lift Membrane Bioreactors (AnMBRs) are a relatively recent development, gaining prominence in the **late 1990s and 2000s** as membrane technology advanced and became more cost-effective.

![**Fig. Air Lift Membrane Bioreactors**](Unit I 1a53a995df8780f69720e68a76b33240/image%209.png)

**Fig. Air Lift Membrane Bioreactors**

**1.4.1.1 Principles and Process Design**

**Principles of Air Lift MBRs:**

- **Anaerobic Digestion and Membrane Filtration:** AnMBRs combine anaerobic digestion with membrane filtration in a single reactor. Anaerobic digestion breaks down organic pollutants in the absence of oxygen, producing biogas and stabilized sludge. Membrane filtration (typically ultrafiltration or microfiltration) is used to separate the treated effluent from the anaerobic biomass.
- **Complete Biomass Retention:** Membrane filtration provides a physical barrier that completely retains the anaerobic biomass within the reactor. This allows for high biomass concentrations and long Solids Retention Times (SRTs), enhancing treatment efficiency and stability.
- **High Effluent Quality:** Membrane filtration produces a high-quality effluent free of suspended solids and bacteria, suitable for reuse or discharge to sensitive environments.
- **Air Lift Circulation:** Air lift is used to circulate the mixed liquor within the reactor and to reduce membrane fouling. Air is injected at the bottom of a riser section, creating buoyancy and upward flow. The mixed liquor then flows downward in a downcomer section, creating circulation. Air lift provides gentle mixing and reduces energy consumption compared to mechanical mixers.

**Process Design of Air Lift MBRs:**

A typical Air Lift MBR system consists of the following components:

1. **Anaerobic Reactor with Membrane Module:** The core of the AnMBR system where anaerobic digestion and membrane filtration occur.
    - **Reactor Configuration:** Typically a cylindrical or rectangular tank.
    - **Membrane Module:** Submerged membrane modules (e.g., hollow fiber or flat sheet) are immersed directly in the anaerobic reactor.
    - **Air Lift System:** Air diffusers are located at the bottom of a riser section to create air lift circulation.
    - **Biogas Collection System:** A headspace above the liquid level to collect biogas produced during anaerobic digestion.
2. **Pre-treatment (Optional):** Pre-treatment (e.g., screening, grit removal) may be needed depending on the characteristics of the influent wastewater.
3. **Permeate Pumping System:** Pumps to draw permeate (treated effluent) through the membrane.
4. **Biogas Handling System:** System for collecting, processing, and utilizing biogas.
5. **Chemical Dosing System (Optional):** Chemical dosing (e.g., for pH control, nutrient supplementation, or membrane cleaning) may be needed.

**Process Design Parameters for Air Lift MBRs:**

- **Hydraulic Retention Time (HRT):** Typically ranges from 12 to 48 hours, depending on wastewater characteristics and treatment goals.
- **Solids Retention Time (SRT):** SRT is decoupled from HRT in MBRs and can be operated at long SRTs (e.g., 20-50 days or longer) to enhance anaerobic digestion and reduce sludge production.
- **Organic Loading Rate (OLR):** OLR is a key design parameter for anaerobic digestion. It depends on the wastewater characteristics and desired treatment efficiency.
- **Membrane Flux:** Membrane flux is typically lower in AnMBRs compared to aerobic MBRs due to higher solids concentrations and fouling potential. Flux rates typically range from 5 to 20 L/m²·h.
- **Transmembrane Pressure (TMP):** TMP is monitored to assess membrane fouling. Cleaning is triggered when TMP reaches a threshold value.
- **Air Flow Rate (for Air Lift):** Air flow rate is optimized for mixing, membrane fouling control, and energy efficiency.
- **Membrane Pore Size:** Typically ultrafiltration (UF) or microfiltration (MF) membranes are used with pore sizes ranging from 0.01 to 0.2 µm.

**1.4.1.2 Operational Parameters and Control**

**Key Operational Parameters for Air Lift MBRs:**

- **Temperature:** Maintain temperature within the mesophilic (30-40°C) or thermophilic (50-60°C) range for anaerobic digestion. Mesophilic conditions are more common for AnMBRs.
- **pH:** Maintain pH in the neutral range (6.5-7.5) for optimal methanogenesis.
- **Volatile Fatty Acids (VFAs):** Monitor VFA concentration as an indicator of process stability.
- **Alkalinity:** Monitor alkalinity to ensure adequate buffering capacity.
- **Hydraulic Retention Time (HRT):** Control HRT by adjusting influent flow rate.
- **Organic Loading Rate (OLR):** Control OLR by adjusting influent organic concentration and flow rate.
- **Solids Retention Time (SRT):** Control SRT by adjusting sludge wastage rate (WAS). In AnMBRs, SRT is typically long, and WAS rate is low.
- **Membrane Flux and TMP:** Monitor membrane flux and TMP. High TMP indicates membrane fouling.
- **Air Flow Rate (for Air Lift):** Optimize air flow rate for mixing and fouling control while minimizing energy consumption.
- **Biogas Production Rate and Composition:** Monitor biogas production rate and methane content as indicators of process performance.

**Control Strategies for Air Lift MBRs:**

- **Temperature Control:** Maintain constant temperature using heating systems if needed.
- **pH Control:** Automated pH control systems with pH probes and chemical dosing pumps.
- **Membrane Fouling Control:**
    - **Backwashing:** Regular backwashing of membranes to remove accumulated foulants.
    - **Chemical Cleaning:** Periodic chemical cleaning (e.g., using sodium hypochlorite or citric acid) to remove stubborn fouling layers.
    - **Air Scouring:** Continuous or intermittent air scouring to reduce fouling.
    - **Relaxation:** Periodic relaxation periods (stopping permeate extraction) to reduce fouling.
- **Biogas Monitoring and Utilization:** Online biogas analyzers and systems for biogas utilization (e.g., for heat and power generation).
- **Process Monitoring and Control Software:** SCADA systems for real-time monitoring and control of AnMBR parameters.
- **Troubleshooting and Problem Solving:** Operators need to be trained to troubleshoot common AnMBR problems like membrane fouling, process instability, and biogas production issues.

### **1.4.2 Packed Bed (Column) Reactors**

Packed Bed Reactors (PBRs) have been used in various chemical and biochemical processes for decades. Their application in anaerobic wastewater treatment, particularly in column reactor configuration, became more prominent in the **1980s and 1990s**.

![Fig. Schematic (A) and picture of the packed bed column (B). Sensors recording data are shown in circles.](Unit I 1a53a995df8780f69720e68a76b33240/image%2010.png)

Fig. Schematic (A) and picture of the packed bed column (B). Sensors recording data are shown in circles.

**1.4.2.1 Principles and Process Design**

**Principles of Packed Bed Reactors:**

- **Biofilm-Based Anaerobic Treatment:** Packed bed reactors are biofilm-based anaerobic treatment systems. Microorganisms grow as a biofilm on the surface of packing media within a column reactor.
- **Attached Growth System:** Similar to RBCs, PBRs are attached growth systems. Microorganisms are immobilized on a fixed medium, providing high biomass concentrations and long SRTs.
- **Upflow or Downflow Operation:** Wastewater can be fed to the reactor in upflow or downflow mode. Upflow is more common for anaerobic PBRs to promote better contact between wastewater and biomass and to facilitate biogas removal.
- **Plug Flow or Mixed Flow:** Depending on the design and flow rate, PBRs can operate in plug flow or mixed flow regimes. Plug flow is generally more efficient for substrate removal but can be more susceptible to clogging.
- **Biogas Production and Removal:** Biogas produced during anaerobic digestion is collected at the top of the reactor.

**Process Design of Packed Bed (Column) Reactors:**

A typical Anaerobic Packed Bed Reactor system consists of the following components:

1. **Column Reactor:** A vertical column packed with inert media to provide a surface for biofilm growth.
    - **Column Material:** Typically made of PVC, stainless steel, or concrete.
    - **Packing Media:** Various types of packing media can be used, including:
        - **Random Packing:** Small, irregularly shaped media (e.g., rocks, gravel, plastic rings, ceramic saddles) randomly packed in the column.
        - **Structured Packing:** Regularly shaped media arranged in a specific pattern to provide uniform flow distribution and high surface area (e.g., corrugated sheets, mesh packing).
    - **Media Size and Surface Area:** Media size and surface area are selected to optimize biofilm growth, mass transfer, and prevent clogging. Smaller media size provides higher surface area but increases clogging potential.
    - **Wastewater Distribution System:** A system at the bottom of the column to distribute influent wastewater uniformly across the packing media.
    - **Effluent Collection System:** A system at the top of the column to collect treated effluent.
    - **Biogas Collection System:** A headspace at the top of the column to collect biogas.
2. **Pre-treatment (Optional):** Pre-treatment (e.g., screening, grit removal, sedimentation) may be needed depending on the characteristics of the influent wastewater.
3. **Effluent Post-treatment (Optional):** Effluent from anaerobic PBRs may require post-treatment (e.g., aerobic polishing, tertiary treatment) to meet stringent discharge standards.
4. **Recirculation System (Optional):** Effluent recirculation can be used to improve mixing, reduce clogging, and enhance treatment efficiency, especially for high-strength wastewater.

**Process Design Parameters for Packed Bed Reactors:**

- **Hydraulic Loading Rate (HLR):** The flow rate of wastewater per unit cross-sectional area of the reactor. HLR typically ranges from 0.1 to 1 m/h for anaerobic PBRs.
- **Organic Loading Rate (OLR):** The mass of COD or BOD applied per unit volume of reactor per unit time. OLR is a key design parameter for anaerobic digestion.
- **Reactor Height-to-Diameter Ratio (H/D):** Typically high H/D ratios are used to promote plug flow and enhance treatment efficiency.
- **Packing Media Type and Surface Area:** Selected based on wastewater characteristics, desired treatment efficiency, and clogging potential.
- **Void Fraction:** The fraction of void space in the packed bed. Void fraction influences flow distribution and clogging potential.
- **Hydraulic Residence Time (HRT):** HRT is determined by reactor volume and flow rate. HRT typically ranges from hours to days for anaerobic PBRs.

**1.4.2.2 Operational Parameters and Control**

**Key Operational Parameters for Packed Bed Reactors:**

- **Temperature:** Maintain temperature within the mesophilic (30-40°C) or thermophilic (50-60°C) range for anaerobic digestion. Mesophilic conditions are more common.
- **pH:** Maintain pH in the neutral range (6.5-7.5) for optimal methanogenesis.
- **Volatile Fatty Acids (VFAs):** Monitor VFA concentration as an indicator of process stability.
- **Alkalinity:** Monitor alkalinity to ensure adequate buffering capacity.
- **Hydraulic Loading Rate (HLR):** Control HLR by adjusting influent flow rate.
- **Organic Loading Rate (OLR):** Control OLR by adjusting influent organic concentration and flow rate.
- **Recirculation Ratio (if recirculation is used):** Optimize recirculation ratio for mixing, clogging control, and treatment efficiency.
- **Biogas Production Rate and Composition:** Monitor biogas production rate and methane content as indicators of process performance.
- **Pressure Drop:** Monitor pressure drop across the packed bed. Increasing pressure drop can indicate clogging.

**Control Strategies for Packed Bed Reactors:**

- **Temperature Control:** Maintain constant temperature using heating systems if needed.
- **pH Control:** Automated pH control systems with pH probes and chemical dosing pumps.
- **Flow Control:** Maintain constant or controlled flow rate to maintain design HLR.
- **Recirculation Control (if recirculation is used):** Adjust recirculation rate based on process monitoring.
- **Backwashing or Media Cleaning (if clogging occurs):** Periodic backwashing or media cleaning may be needed to remove accumulated solids and biomass and to alleviate clogging.
- **Biogas Monitoring and Utilization:** Online biogas analyzers and systems for biogas utilization.
- **Process Monitoring and Control Software:** SCADA systems for real-time monitoring and control of PBR parameters.
- **Troubleshooting and Problem Solving:** Operators need to be trained to troubleshoot common PBR problems like clogging, process instability, and reduced treatment efficiency.
