## 3.1 Antibacterial Drugs

### 3.1.1 Sulfonamides

Sulfonamides, or sulfa drugs, are a class of synthetic antibacterial agents that have historically played a critical role in the treatment of bacterial infections. Their introduction marked a significant advancement in antimicrobial therapy, and they continue to be relevant in specific clinical contexts today.

**Structure**

Sulfonamides are characterized by the presence of a sulfonamide functional group (-SO2NH2) attached to an aromatic amine. The general structure can be represented as follows:

- **Chemical Formula**: C₆H₈N₂O₂S
- **Molecular Structure**:
    - The core structure consists of a benzene ring substituted with an amino group (-NH₂) and a sulfonamide group (-SO₂NH₂).

![Fig.  General structure of sulphonamides; R=R1=H for sulphanilamide.](Unit III 1a63a995df8780c3ae22d5b53434e624/image.png)

Fig.  General structure of sulphonamides; R=R1=H for sulphanilamide.

The most common representative of this class is **sulfanilamide**, which serves as the prototype for many derivatives used in clinical practice.

**Mechanism of Action**

The primary mechanism of action for sulfonamides is their ability to act as competitive inhibitors of the enzyme **dihydropteroate synthase (DHPS)**, which is crucial for bacterial folate synthesis.

![Fig. Mechanism of Action of Sulfonamides](Unit III 1a63a995df8780c3ae22d5b53434e624/image%202.png)

Fig. Mechanism of Action of Sulfonamides

1. **Folate Synthesis Pathway**:
    - Bacteria synthesize folate from para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) through a series of enzymatic reactions.
    - DHPS catalyzes the condensation of PABA with p-hydroxybenzoate (DHP) to form dihydropteroate, an immediate precursor in folate biosynthesis.
2. **Inhibition Mechanism**:
    - Sulfonamides mimic PABA due to their structural similarity, allowing them to competitively bind to the active site of DHPS.
    - By occupying this site, sulfonamides prevent the formation of dihydropteroate, thereby inhibiting folate synthesis.
    - Without sufficient folate, bacteria cannot synthesize nucleic acids and proteins essential for growth and replication, leading to growth inhibition (bacteriostatic effect).
3. **Selective Toxicity**:
    - Humans obtain folate through dietary sources and do not synthesize it, which allows sulfonamides to selectively target bacterial cells without affecting human cells.

**Applications**

Sulfonamides have a broad spectrum of activity against both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, as well as certain protozoa. Their applications include:

1. **Treatment of Infections**:
    - Sulfonamides are effective against various infections, including urinary tract infections (UTIs), respiratory infections, and certain gastrointestinal infections.
    - They are also used in treating infections caused by *Nocardia*, *Actinomyces*, and some protozoal infections like coccidiosis and toxoplasmosis.
2. **Combination Therapy**:
    - Sulfonamides are often used in combination with diaminopyrimidines (e.g., trimethoprim) to enhance efficacy through synergistic effects.
    - This combination leads to a sequential blockade of the folate synthesis pathway, resulting in bactericidal activity rather than just bacteriostatic effects.
3. **Veterinary Medicine**:
    - Due to their low cost and effectiveness, sulfonamides remain widely used in veterinary medicine for treating bacterial infections in livestock and pets.
4. **Antimicrobial Stewardship**:
    - While effective, the use of sulfonamides has declined due to the emergence of resistance and the availability of newer antibiotics. However, they still play a role in specific clinical scenarios where resistance patterns favour their use.

**Resistance Mechanisms**

Despite their historical significance and effectiveness, resistance to sulfonamides has emerged due to various mechanisms:

1. **Mutations in DHPS**:
    - Bacteria can develop mutations in the DHPS enzyme that reduce binding affinity for sulfonamides, rendering them ineffective.
2. **Increased Production of PABA**:
    - Some bacteria can compensate for sulfonamide inhibition by overproducing PABA, effectively out-competing the drug for binding to DHPS.
3. **Acquisition of Resistance Genes**:
    - Horizontal gene transfer can introduce resistance genes that encode altered forms of DHPS or other enzymes involved in folate metabolism.
4. **Alternative Pathways**:
    - Some bacteria may develop alternative metabolic pathways that bypass the need for folate synthesis altogether.

**Pharmacokinetics**

- **Absorption**: Sulfonamides are well absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract after oral administration.
- **Distribution**: They are widely distributed throughout body tissues and fluids, including pleural, peritoneal, synovial, and ocular fluids.
- **Metabolism**: Sulfonamides undergo hepatic metabolism; some metabolites may retain antibacterial activity.
- **Excretion**: Primarily excreted via renal pathways; they can crystallize in urine if not adequately hydrated due to their low solubility.

**Adverse Effects**

Common adverse effects associated with sulfonamide use include:

- **Hypersensitivity Reactions**: Rash, hives, or more severe reactions such as Stevens-Johnson syndrome.
- **Hematological Effects**: Agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia, or hemolytic anaemia.
- **Renal Toxicity**: Risk of crystalluria leading to kidney damage if adequate hydration is not maintained.
- **Gastrointestinal Disturbances**: Nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea may occur as side effects.

### 3.1.2 Quinolones

Quinolones are a crucial class of synthetic antibacterial agents, highly valued for their broad-spectrum activity and efficacy in treating a wide array of bacterial infections in both human and veterinary medicine. Their discovery and development have significantly advanced antimicrobial therapy.

**Structure** 

The quinolone class is characterized by a fundamental bicyclic structure, the quinolone ring system, which is indispensable for their antibacterial action. Modifications to this core structure dictate their pharmacological properties.

![Fig. Basic Structure of All Quinolones](Unit III 1a63a995df8780c3ae22d5b53434e624/image%201.png)

Fig. Basic Structure of All Quinolones

- **Core Structure: The Quinolone Ring System:** The foundational quinolone structure is composed of two fused rings: a benzene ring fused to a pyridine ring. Crucially, it features a nitrogen atom at position 1 and a keto group at position 4. This basic scaffold is essential for interaction with the target enzymes.
- **Substitutions and Structure-Activity Relationships (SAR):** The quinolone ring is amenable to various chemical substitutions at different positions. These modifications are strategically employed to fine-tune the drug's characteristics:
    - **Position 6 Fluorine (F):** The introduction of a fluorine atom at position 6 is a hallmark of fluoroquinolones and a critical determinant of enhanced antibacterial potency, especially against Gram-negative bacteria. Fluorination improves cell penetration and target binding.
    - **N-1 Substitutions:** Substitutions at the nitrogen at position 1, often with a cyclopropyl or ethyl group, enhance activity and pharmacokinetic properties.
    - **C-7 Substitutions:** Modifications at carbon 7, often involving piperazine or pyrrolidine rings, influence the spectrum of activity and potency against Gram-positive bacteria and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.
    - **C-8 Substitutions:** Substitutions at carbon 8, such as the addition of a methoxy group (as in moxifloxacin), can improve activity against Gram-positive bacteria and reduce photosensitivity.
    - **C-3 Carboxylic Acid and C-4 Keto Group:** The carboxylic acid at position 3 and the keto group at position 4 are essential for chelating divalent cations and for enzyme binding.
- **Fluoroquinolones: A Potent Subgroup:** Fluoroquinolones, distinguished by the presence of a fluorine atom at position 6, represent a major and clinically important subgroup. Examples of prominent fluoroquinolones include:
    - **Ciprofloxacin:** Known for its excellent Gram-negative activity, often used for UTIs and respiratory infections.
    - **Levofloxacin:** A broader spectrum fluoroquinolone, effective against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, and atypicals.
    - **Moxifloxacin:** Exhibits enhanced activity against Gram-positive bacteria and anaerobes, with good respiratory penetration.

**Mechanism of Action** 

Quinolones exert their bactericidal effects by disrupting bacterial DNA replication and repair. They achieve this by targeting two essential bacterial enzymes, DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV:

![Fig. Mechanism of Action - Quinolones](Unit III 1a63a995df8780c3ae22d5b53434e624/image%203.png)

Fig. Mechanism of Action - Quinolones

- **Dual Target Enzymes:**
    - **DNA Gyrase (Topoisomerase II):** The primary target in Gram-negative bacteria. DNA gyrase is a type II topoisomerase uniquely present in bacteria. It is ATP-dependent and essential for introducing negative supercoils into bacterial DNA. Negative supercoiling is crucial for DNA compaction, replication initiation, transcription, and repair.
    - **Topoisomerase IV:** The primary target in many Gram-positive bacteria and also relevant in Gram-negatives. Topoisomerase IV is a type II topoisomerase that plays a critical role in decatenating (separating) newly replicated daughter DNA molecules. Decatenation is essential for proper chromosome segregation during bacterial cell division.
- **Detailed Mechanism of Inhibition: Stabilization of Cleavage Complexes:**
    - **Drug Binding and DNA Cleavage:** Quinolones enter bacterial cells and form a ternary complex with the topoisomerase enzyme and DNA. They bind to the enzyme-DNA complex after the enzyme has cleaved the DNA, but before religation.
    - **Prevention of Religation:** Quinolones prevent the religation step of the topoisomerase catalytic cycle. This trapping of the enzyme in a cleaved state is termed "stabilization of cleavage complexes."
    - **Irreversible Double-Strand Breaks and Cell Death:** The stabilized cleavage complexes are not readily reversible and lead to persistent double-strand breaks in the bacterial chromosome. These double-strand breaks are highly toxic and trigger bacterial cell death pathways. The bactericidal action of quinolones is directly linked to these drug-induced DNA breaks.
- **Selective Toxicity: Exploiting Bacterial Enzyme Specificity:** Quinolones exhibit remarkable selective toxicity because bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV are structurally and mechanistically distinct from mammalian topoisomerases (Topoisomerase IIα and IIβ in humans). While quinolones can, at very high concentrations, interact with human topoisomerases (particularly Topoisomerase IIα), clinically relevant concentrations achieve effective bacterial enzyme inhibition with minimal impact on human cells. This differential targeting underpins their safety profile at therapeutic doses.

**Applications** 

Fluoroquinolones, owing to their broad spectrum and favorable pharmacokinetic properties, are clinically valuable for treating diverse bacterial infections. Specific examples include:

- **Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs):** Highly effective against common uropathogens such as *Escherichia coli*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Proteus mirabilis*, and *Staphylococcus saprophyticus*. Agents like ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin are frequently used.
- **Respiratory Tract Infections:**
    - **Pneumonia:** Effective against community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), including infections caused by *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*, and atypical pathogens like *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* and *Legionella pneumophila*. Levofloxacin and moxifloxacin are often preferred for CAP due to enhanced activity against *S. pneumoniae*.
    - **Bronchitis and Sinusitis:** Used in acute exacerbations of chronic bronchitis and acute bacterial sinusitis, particularly when caused by resistant organisms or when other first-line agents are unsuitable.
- **Prostatitis:** Quinolones, especially ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin, achieve excellent penetration into prostate tissue, making them highly effective for bacterial prostatitis.
- **Skin and Soft Tissue Infections:** Effective for skin infections, including cellulitis, erysipelas, and wound infections. Useful for infections involving *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, particularly ciprofloxacin.
- **Intra-abdominal Infections:** Used in combination regimens with other antibiotics (e.g., metronidazole) for the treatment of complicated intra-abdominal infections, including diverticulitis and peritonitis, to cover a broad spectrum of aerobic and anaerobic bacteria.
- **Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs):** Historically used for gonorrhea and chlamydia. However, due to increasing resistance in *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*, quinolones are no longer recommended as first-line agents for gonorrhea in many regions. They may still be used for chlamydial infections and chancroid.
- **Bone and Joint Infections (Osteomyelitis):** Excellent bone penetration makes them valuable for treating osteomyelitis caused by susceptible organisms, including *P. aeruginosa* and *Serratia marcescens*.
- **Anthrax:** Ciprofloxacin is a critical first-line agent recommended by public health agencies for post-exposure prophylaxis and treatment of inhalational and cutaneous anthrax caused by *Bacillus anthracis*.
- **Traveler's Diarrhea:** Effective for treating bacterial traveler's diarrhea, particularly when caused by *E. coli*, *Campylobacter jejuni*, or *Shigella* species.

**Resistance Mechanisms** 

The escalating prevalence of quinolone resistance poses a significant clinical challenge. Resistance arises through several key mechanisms:

- **Mutations in Target Genes: Chromosomal Resistance:**
    - **GyrA and GyrB (DNA Gyrase Subunits):** Mutations in the *gyrA* and *gyrB* genes, encoding the subunits of DNA gyrase, are the most frequent mechanism of quinolone resistance in Gram-negative bacteria (e.g., *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Pseudomonas*). These mutations typically cluster in a region of *gyrA* known as the Quinolone Resistance-Determining Region (QRDR), and less frequently in *gyrB*. They alter the quinolone-binding pocket within DNA gyrase, reducing the affinity of quinolones for the enzyme and thus diminishing drug efficacy. *gyrA* mutations are often associated with higher levels of resistance.
    - **ParC and ParE (Topoisomerase IV Subunits):** Mutations in the *parC* and *parE* genes, encoding the subunits of topoisomerase IV, are more commonly observed in Gram-positive bacteria (e.g., *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Staphylococcus aureus*). Similar to *gyr* mutations, *par* mutations occur in the QRDR of these genes and reduce quinolone binding to topoisomerase IV. In *S. pneumoniae*, *parC* mutations are often the primary mechanism, while in *S. aureus*, both *gyrA* and *grlA* (*parC* homologue in *S. aureus*) mutations are important.
    - **Stepwise Accumulation of Mutations:** Resistance often develops through the sequential accumulation of mutations in both *gyrA/gyrB* and *parC/parE*. Each mutation incrementally reduces quinolone susceptibility, leading to high-level resistance.
- **Efflux Pumps: Active Drug Export:** Overexpression of bacterial efflux pumps, particularly multidrug efflux pumps, is a significant contributor to quinolone resistance in both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. These pumps actively transport quinolones out of the bacterial cell, lowering intracellular drug concentrations below the therapeutic threshold.
    - **Examples:** The AcrAB-TolC efflux system in *E. coli* and the NorA pump in *Staphylococcus aureus* are well-characterized efflux pumps mediating quinolone resistance. Regulatory mutations that upregulate the expression of these pumps contribute to resistance.
- **Plasmid-Mediated Resistance: Horizontal Gene Transfer:** Plasmid-borne quinolone resistance genes facilitate the rapid spread of resistance through horizontal gene transfer.
    - ***qnr* Genes (Quinolone Resistance):** *qnr* genes encode proteins that belong to the pentapeptide repeat family. These Qnr proteins protect DNA gyrase from quinolone inhibition, likely by physically interacting with gyrase and altering its conformation to reduce quinolone binding. Several *qnr* variants exist (*qnrA*, *qnrB*, *qnrC*, *qnrD*, *qnrS*), often found on plasmids and contributing to low-level resistance, but can facilitate the selection of high-level chromosomal mutations.
    - ***aac(6')-Ib-cr* Gene (Aminoglycoside Acetyltransferase):** This plasmid-borne gene encodes a variant of aminoglycoside acetyltransferase that can acetylate ciprofloxacin and norfloxacin, reducing their antibacterial activity. This mechanism confers resistance specifically to ciprofloxacin and norfloxacin but not necessarily to other fluoroquinolones.
- **Target Protection Mechanisms:**
    - ***MfpA* Protein (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*):** *MfpA* is a protein found in *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* that confers quinolone resistance through a unique mechanism of target protection. MfpA mimics B-form DNA and binds to DNA gyrase, preventing quinolone binding and inhibition.

**Pharmacokinetics** 

The pharmacokinetic properties of quinolones are generally favorable, contributing to their clinical utility.

- **Absorption: Oral Bioavailability and Factors Affecting Absorption:** Quinolones are generally well absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract following oral administration, typically achieving bioavailability of 70-95%. However, absorption can be significantly impaired by:
    - **Divalent and Trivalent Cations:** Co-administration with products containing divalent and trivalent cations (e.g., antacids containing aluminum or magnesium hydroxide, calcium supplements, iron supplements, zinc) can significantly reduce quinolone absorption. These cations chelate with quinolones in the gut lumen, forming poorly absorbed complexes. Patients should be advised to separate quinolone administration from these products by at least 2-4 hours.
    - **Dairy Products:** Dairy products contain calcium, which can similarly interfere with absorption.
- **Distribution: Tissue Penetration and Intracellular Accumulation:** Quinolones exhibit excellent distribution into various body tissues and fluids, often exceeding serum concentrations in certain tissues.
    - **Excellent Tissue Penetration:** Achieve high concentrations in prostate tissue, bone, lung tissue, kidney, and bile. This excellent tissue penetration is crucial for treating infections in these sites.
    - **Intracellular Penetration:** Quinolones penetrate well into cells, including phagocytes (macrophages, neutrophils). This intracellular penetration enhances their efficacy against intracellular bacteria (e.g., *Legionella*, *Chlamydia*, *Mycoplasma*).
    - **Volume of Distribution (Vd):** Typically exhibit large volumes of distribution, reflecting their extensive tissue penetration.
- **Metabolism: Hepatic Metabolism and Drug Interactions:** The extent of hepatic metabolism varies among different quinolones.
    - **Variable Hepatic Metabolism:** Some quinolones (e.g., ciprofloxacin) undergo moderate hepatic metabolism, involving cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP450), particularly CYP1A2. Others (e.g., levofloxacin, moxifloxacin) are primarily excreted unchanged renally, with minimal hepatic metabolism.
    - **Drug Interactions:** Quinolones metabolized by CYP450 enzymes can be involved in drug interactions. For example, ciprofloxacin can inhibit CYP1A2, potentially increasing the levels of drugs metabolized by this enzyme (e.g., theophylline, warfarin).
- **Excretion: Renal and Biliary Routes and Renal Adjustment:** The primary route of excretion is renal, mainly through glomerular filtration and active tubular secretion. Some quinolones also undergo biliary excretion.
    - **Renal Excretion Predominant:** Renal clearance is the major elimination pathway for most quinolones.
    - **Renal Dosage Adjustment:** Dosage adjustments are crucial in patients with renal impairment to prevent drug accumulation and toxicity. Dosage adjustments are typically based on creatinine clearance.
    - **Biliary Excretion (Moxifloxacin):** Moxifloxacin exhibits a higher degree of biliary excretion compared to other quinolones, making it potentially useful in patients with renal dysfunction, although dosage adjustment may still be needed in severe hepatic impairment.

**Adverse Effects** 

While generally well-tolerated, quinolones are associated with a range of potential adverse effects that clinicians must be aware of:

- **Gastrointestinal Disturbances:** Common but usually mild.
    - **Symptoms:** Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, dyspepsia. These effects are often due to gastrointestinal irritation.
    - **Management:** Taking quinolones with food may help reduce gastrointestinal upset.
- **Central Nervous System (CNS) Effects:** Can occur due to quinolone penetration into the CNS.
    - **Symptoms:** Headache, dizziness, lightheadedness, insomnia, restlessness, anxiety, confusion, and rarely, seizures. Seizures are more likely in patients with pre-existing seizure disorders or when high doses are used.
    - **Caution in Epilepsy:** Quinolones should be used with caution in patients with epilepsy or other CNS disorders.
- **QT Prolongation and Cardiovascular Risks:** Some quinolones, particularly moxifloxacin and levofloxacin, can prolong the QT interval on electrocardiogram (ECG).
    - **Mechanism:** QT prolongation is due to blockade of cardiac potassium channels (hERG channels).
    - **Risk of Arrhythmias:** QT prolongation increases the risk of potentially life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias, such as torsades de pointes, especially in susceptible individuals.
    - **Risk Factors:** Pre-existing QT prolongation, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, bradycardia, heart failure, and concomitant use of other QT-prolonging drugs increase the risk.
    - **ECG Monitoring:** ECG monitoring may be warranted in patients at high risk for QT prolongation, particularly with moxifloxacin.
- **Tendon Rupture and Tendinitis:** A rare but serious and potentially debilitating adverse effect.
    - **Mechanism:** The exact mechanism is not fully understood but may involve quinolone-induced oxidative stress, matrix metalloproteinase activation, and disruption of collagen synthesis in tendons.
    - **Risk Factors:** Older age (>60 years), corticosteroid use, renal failure, and transplant recipients are at increased risk.
    - **Achilles Tendon Most Commonly Affected:** Achilles tendon rupture is the most frequently reported tendon injury, but other tendons can also be affected.
    - **Symptoms:** Tendon pain, swelling, inflammation, and sudden rupture. Symptoms can occur during treatment or even several months after discontinuation.
    - **Discontinuation and Avoidance:** Quinolones should be discontinued at the first sign of tendinitis or tendon rupture. Patients with risk factors should be prescribed quinolones cautiously, and alternative antibiotics should be considered when possible.
- **Peripheral Neuropathy:** Quinolones can cause peripheral neuropathy, which can be irreversible in some cases.
    - **Symptoms:** Numbness, tingling, burning pain, weakness in the extremities.
    - **Irreversibility:** In some patients, peripheral neuropathy may persist long after drug discontinuation or become permanent.
    - **Discontinuation:** Quinolones should be discontinued immediately if symptoms of neuropathy develop.
- **Photosensitivity:** Increased sensitivity to ultraviolet (UV) radiation from sunlight or tanning beds.
    - **Mechanism:** Quinolones can accumulate in the skin and enhance skin's reactivity to UV light.
    - **Risk of Sunburn:** Patients are at increased risk of sunburn even with brief sun exposure.
    - **Sun Protection:** Patients should be advised to take precautions to avoid excessive sun exposure and use sunscreen during quinolone therapy.
- **Dysglycemia:** Quinolones can cause disturbances in blood glucose levels, including both hypoglycemia (low blood sugar) and hyperglycemia (high blood sugar).
    - **Risk in Diabetics:** Diabetic patients, especially those treated with insulin or sulfonylureas, are at increased risk of dysglycemia.
    - **Monitoring Blood Glucose:** Blood glucose monitoring is recommended in diabetic patients receiving quinolones.
- ***Clostridioides difficile* Infection (CDI):** As broad-spectrum antibiotics, quinolones can disrupt the normal gut microbiota, creating an opportunity for *Clostridioides difficile* to proliferate and cause CDI.
    - **Risk of CDI:** Quinolone use is associated with an increased risk of CDI, ranging from mild diarrhea to severe pseudomembranous colitis.
    - **Consider CDI in Diarrhea:** CDI should be considered in any patient who develops diarrhea during or after quinolone therapy.

**Clinical Considerations** 

Judicious use and careful consideration of patient factors are essential for optimizing quinolone therapy and mitigating risks.

- **Resistance Monitoring and Local Epidemiology:** Due to the increasing global prevalence of quinolone resistance, it is crucial to:
    - **Monitor Local Resistance Patterns:** Clinicians should be aware of local quinolone resistance rates for common pathogens in their region. Local antibiograms and susceptibility data should guide empirical therapy.
    - **Culture and Susceptibility Testing:** When possible, obtain bacterial cultures and perform susceptibility testing to guide targeted quinolone therapy and avoid unnecessary use in resistant infections.
- **Appropriate Use and Antimicrobial Stewardship:** To preserve the effectiveness of quinolones and minimize resistance development, they should be used judiciously and reserved for situations where:
    - **Clear Indications:** Use quinolones only for infections where they are clearly indicated based on established guidelines and susceptibility patterns.
    - **Alternative Agents Not Suitable:** Reserve quinolones for infections where first-line agents (e.g., beta-lactams, macrolides) are not effective, contraindicated, or less desirable due to resistance or allergy.
    - **Avoid Routine Use for Uncomplicated Infections:** Avoid routine use of quinolones for uncomplicated infections (e.g., uncomplicated UTIs in women) where narrower-spectrum agents are effective.
- **Patient Counseling and Risk Mitigation:** Thorough patient counseling is essential to minimize adverse effects and ensure safe quinolone use. Counseling should include:
    - **Adverse Effects Awareness:** Inform patients about potential adverse effects, particularly tendon rupture, peripheral neuropathy, QT prolongation, and photosensitivity, and instruct them to report any concerning symptoms promptly.
    - **Drug Interactions:** Advise patients about potential drug interactions, especially with antacids, supplements containing divalent or trivalent cations, and other QT-prolonging drugs.
    - **Administration Instructions:** Provide clear instructions on how to take quinolones, including timing relative to meals and cation-containing products.
    - **Sun Protection:** Advise patients to take sun protection measures to minimize photosensitivity reactions.

## 3.2 Antiviral Drugs

### 3.2.1 Amantadine

Amantadine, chemically designated as 1-adamantanamine hydrochloride, is an antiviral and antiparkinsonian drug, marketed under various brand names including Gocovri. It is a derivative of adamantane, a unique, cage-like hydrocarbon molecule. Rimantadine is a closely related analog with similar antiviral properties.

![Fig. Structures of amantadine (Symmetrel ), rimantadine](Unit III 1a63a995df8780c3ae22d5b53434e624/image%204.png)

Fig. Structures of amantadine (Symmetrel ), rimantadine

**Structure**

Amantadine's structure is built upon an adamantane scaffold, a rigid, symmetrical, tricyclic hydrocarbon.

- **Adamantane Core:** The core of amantadine is adamantane (C₁₀H₁₆), a diamondoid molecule composed of three fused cyclohexane rings arranged in a cage-like structure. This rigid, lipophilic structure is crucial for its interaction with viral and host cell proteins.
- **Amino Substitution:** Amantadine is specifically 1-adamantanamine, indicating the substitution of one of the four tertiary carbon atoms of adamantane with an amino group (-NH₂). In its clinically used form, it is typically the hydrochloride salt (·HCl), enhancing its water solubility for pharmaceutical formulations.
- **Rimantadine Analog:** Rimantadine, or α-methyl-1-adamantanemethylamine, is a closely related analog. It shares the adamantane core but has an additional methyl group and a methylamine substituent. While structurally similar, rimantadine exhibits slightly different pharmacokinetic properties and may have a milder side-effect profile compared to amantadine.
- **Targeting M2 Protein:** Both amantadine and rimantadine are uniquely effective against the M2 proton channel of the influenza A virus due to the specific fit and interaction of their adamantane structure within the M2 channel pore.

**Mechanism of Action**

Amantadine's pharmacological actions are multifaceted, encompassing antiviral, antiparkinsonian, and other neurological effects. Its mechanisms are complex and involve interactions with various protein targets.

- **Antiviral Mechanism: M2 Proton Channel Inhibition:** The primary antiviral action of amantadine targets the M2 protein, an integral membrane protein of the influenza A virus.
    - **M2 Protein Function:** The M2 protein functions as a proton-selective ion channel. It is crucial for viral replication by mediating the acidification of the interior of the virion after viral entry into the endosome of the host cell. This acidification is essential for the dissociation of the viral matrix protein from the ribonucleoprotein complex, a necessary step for the release of viral RNA into the cytoplasm and subsequent viral replication.
    - **M2 Channel Blockade:** Amantadine and rimantadine block the M2 proton channel, preventing proton influx into the virion. This inhibition disrupts the pH-dependent uncoating process, trapping the viral genetic material within the virion and preventing viral replication.
    - **Solid-State NMR Insights:** Solid-state Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy studies have provided detailed insights into the amantadine-M2 interaction within lipid bilayers, mimicking the viral membrane environment. These studies reveal two binding sites for amantadine within the M2 channel.
        - **High-Affinity Luminal Binding Site:** The high-affinity site is located in the N-terminal lumen of the M2 channel. A single amantadine molecule occupies this site, surrounded by amino acid residues known to mutate in amantadine-resistant viruses (e.g., Ser31, Val27).
        - **Mechanism of Inhibition: Physical Occlusion and Protonation Equilibrium Perturbation:** Luminal binding suggests that amantadine inhibits M2 function through physical occlusion of the channel pore, thereby interrupting the continuous chain of water molecules ("water wires") necessary for proton conduction. Furthermore, amantadine binding is thought to perturb the protonation equilibrium of key residues within the M2 channel, further disrupting proton transport.
    - **Resistance Mechanisms: M2 Protein Mutations:** Resistance to amantadine and rimantadine rapidly emerges due to mutations in the *M2* gene of influenza A virus. These mutations typically involve amino acid substitutions at positions critical for drug binding within the M2 channel, such as Ser31Asn, Val27Ala, Leu26Phe, and Ala30Thr. These mutations alter the conformation of the M2 channel, reducing the binding affinity of adamantanes and rendering the drugs ineffective. The widespread prevalence of amantadine-resistant influenza A strains has significantly limited the clinical utility of these drugs for influenza treatment.
- **Antiparkinsonian Mechanism: Dopaminergic and Sigma-1 Receptor Modulation:** Amantadine's antiparkinsonian effects are attributed to complex interactions with neurotransmitter systems in the brain, particularly the dopaminergic system.
    - **Dopamine Release Enhancement:** Amantadine promotes the release of dopamine from presynaptic nerve terminals in the brain. This action increases dopamine availability in the synapse, compensating for dopamine deficiency characteristic of Parkinson's disease.
    - **Dopamine Reuptake Inhibition:** Amantadine weakly blocks the reuptake of dopamine by presynaptic neurons. This further contributes to increased dopamine levels in the synaptic cleft, prolonging dopamine's action at receptors.
    - **Dopamine Receptor Stimulation:** Amantadine may directly stimulate dopamine receptors, particularly D2 receptors, although this effect is relatively weak.
    - **Norepinephrine Response Stimulation:** Amantadine is also thought to stimulate norepinephrine neurotransmission, which may contribute to its overall therapeutic effects in Parkinson's disease.
    - **NMDA Receptor Antagonism (Weak):** Amantadine acts as a weak noncompetitive antagonist of NMDA-type glutamate receptors. Excessive glutamatergic neurotransmission is implicated in excitotoxicity and neurodegeneration in Parkinson's disease. NMDA receptor antagonism may provide neuroprotective effects and contribute to symptom relief.
    - **Sigma-1 Receptor Agonism:** Amantadine is a sigma σ₁ receptor agonist. Sigma-1 receptors are endoplasmic reticulum-resident chaperone proteins involved in calcium signaling, neuronal differentiation, and neuroprotection. Sigma-1 receptor agonism is thought to enhance dopaminergic neurotransmission and may contribute to the therapeutic efficacy of amantadine in Parkinson's disease and levodopa-induced dyskinesia.

**Applications**

Amantadine has clinical applications in both neurology and infectious disease, although its use in influenza is now limited by resistance.

- **Parkinson's Disease Management:** Amantadine is used as monotherapy in early, mild Parkinson's disease and as adjunctive therapy in later stages, particularly to manage motor fluctuations and levodopa-induced dyskinesia.
    - **Symptom Relief:** Amantadine can improve the primary motor symptoms of Parkinson's disease, including bradykinesia (slowness of movement), rigidity, and tremor. It is often used to provide symptomatic relief, particularly in the early stages of the disease or in patients experiencing mild symptoms.
    - **Levodopa-Induced Dyskinesia (LID) Treatment:** Amantadine, especially in extended-release formulations, is approved for the treatment of levodopa-induced dyskinesia, a common and debilitating motor complication of long-term levodopa therapy in Parkinson's disease. Amantadine can reduce the severity and duration of involuntary movements associated with LID.
    - **Drug-Induced Extrapyramidal Symptoms:** Amantadine is also used to treat drug-induced extrapyramidal symptoms, such as parkinsonism, dystonia, and akathisia, which can be caused by antipsychotic medications and other drugs that block dopamine receptors.
- **Influenza A Virus Infections (Historically):** Amantadine was initially approved for the treatment and prophylaxis of influenza A virus infections.
    - **Limited Current Use:** Due to the rapid emergence and widespread circulation of amantadine-resistant influenza A virus strains, particularly in subtypes H3N2 and H1N1, amantadine and rimantadine are no longer recommended for routine use in the treatment or prophylaxis of seasonal influenza in many regions globally. Current influenza treatment guidelines emphasize the use of neuraminidase inhibitors (e.g., oseltamivir, zanamivir) and cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitors (e.g., baloxavir marboxil) due to their efficacy against both influenza A and B viruses and lower resistance rates. However, in specific situations where susceptibility is confirmed and alternative antivirals are not available or tolerated, amantadine might be considered under expert guidance.

**Pharmacokinetics**

Amantadine exhibits favorable pharmacokinetic properties, contributing to its oral bioavailability and clinical utility.

- **Absorption and Bioavailability:** Amantadine is well absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract after oral administration. It has high oral bioavailability, typically exceeding 90%, indicating efficient absorption into the systemic circulation. Food intake does not significantly affect its absorption.
- **Distribution:** Amantadine is widely distributed throughout body tissues and fluids. It readily crosses the blood-brain barrier, achieving therapeutic concentrations in the central nervous system, which is essential for both its antiviral and antiparkinsonian actions. The volume of distribution is large, reflecting its extensive tissue penetration.
- **Metabolism:** Amantadine undergoes minimal metabolism in the liver. A small fraction may be acetylated, but the majority of the drug is excreted unchanged. The lack of extensive hepatic metabolism reduces the risk of drug-drug interactions mediated by cytochrome P450 enzymes.
- **Excretion:** Amantadine is primarily eliminated from the body by renal excretion. It is excreted largely unchanged in the urine through glomerular filtration and active tubular secretion.
    - **Renal Clearance:** Renal clearance is the major determinant of amantadine elimination.
    - **Half-Life and Renal Impairment:** The elimination half-life of amantadine is approximately 10 to 30 hours in individuals with normal renal function. However, in patients with renal impairment, particularly end-stage kidney disease, the half-life is significantly prolonged, averaging around eight days. Accumulation of amantadine can occur in renal insufficiency, increasing the risk of adverse effects, especially neurological toxicity.
    - **Dosage Adjustment in Renal Impairment:** Dosage adjustments are crucial in patients with renal impairment. Dosage reduction or延长 dosing intervals are necessary based on creatinine clearance to prevent drug accumulation and toxicity. Amantadine is contraindicated or used with extreme caution in severe renal impairment.

**Side Effects**

Amantadine generally has a manageable side-effect profile, but neurological and anticholinergic effects are noteworthy.

- **Neurological Side Effects:** These are the most common and clinically significant adverse effects of amantadine, likely related to its CNS activity and dopaminergic effects.
    - **Common Neurological Effects:** Drowsiness, insomnia, lightheadedness, dizziness, anxiety, irritability, and confusion are frequently reported.
    - **Cognitive and Psychiatric Effects:** Amantadine can cause cognitive impairment, difficulty concentrating, hallucinations, vivid dreams, and psychosis, particularly in elderly patients or those with pre-existing psychiatric conditions.
    - **Seizures:** Although rare, amantadine can lower the seizure threshold and increase the risk of seizures, especially in patients with epilepsy or a history of seizures.
- **Anticholinergic Side Effects:** Amantadine possesses mild anticholinergic properties, which can contribute to certain side effects.
    - **Dry Mouth:** Reduced salivary secretion leading to dry mouth (xerostomia).
    - **Constipation:** Decreased gastrointestinal motility causing constipation.
    - **Urinary Retention:** Impaired bladder emptying and urinary retention, particularly in older men with prostatic hypertrophy.
    - **Blurred Vision:** Mydriasis (pupil dilation) and blurred vision may occur.
- **Cardiovascular Side Effects:**
    - **Orthostatic Hypotension:** Postural hypotension, a drop in blood pressure upon standing, can occur, increasing the risk of falls, especially in elderly individuals.
    - **Peripheral Edema:** Swelling in the ankles and legs (peripheral edema) can develop.
- **Other Side Effects:**
    - **Livedo Reticularis:** A characteristic but benign skin finding associated with amantadine use, characterized by a net-like purplish discoloration of the skin, particularly on the lower extremities. It is usually reversible upon drug discontinuation.
- **Serious Side Effects:** Although less common, serious side effects can occur, including severe hallucinations, psychosis, seizures, neuroleptic malignant syndrome (rare), and suicidal ideation.
- **Renal Considerations:** Due to its renal excretion, amantadine should be used with caution in patients with renal impairment. Dosage adjustment is essential, and in severe renal insufficiency, amantadine may be contraindicated due to the risk of drug accumulation and neurological toxicity. Regular monitoring of renal function is advisable in patients receiving amantadine, especially in the elderly and those with pre-existing kidney disease.

### 3.2.2 Azidothymidine (AZT)

Azidothymidine (AZT), also widely known as zidovudine (ZDV), is a synthetic thymidine analog and a pivotal nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI).  Chemically, it is 3'-azido-3'-deoxythymidine. AZT was the first antiretroviral drug approved for the treatment of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection and Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS), marking a turning point in the therapeutic approach to this devastating pandemic.

**Structure**

AZT is a synthetic nucleoside analog structurally related to the natural nucleoside thymidine.  Its key structural feature is the azido group at the 3' position.

![Fig. Azidothymidine  along with Thymidine](Unit III 1a63a995df8780c3ae22d5b53434e624/image%205.png)

Fig. Azidothymidine  along with Thymidine

- **Thymidine Analog:** AZT is designed as an analog of thymidine, one of the four nucleosides that constitute DNA. It closely resembles the deoxythymidine nucleoside, which is naturally incorporated into DNA.
- **Azido Group Substitution at 3'-Position:** The defining structural modification in AZT is the substitution of the hydroxyl group (-OH) at the 3' position of the deoxyribose sugar with an azido group (-N₃). This seemingly minor change has profound consequences for its mechanism of action.
- **Absence of 3'-Hydroxyl Group:** The crucial consequence of the azido substitution is the absence of a 3'-hydroxyl group (-OH) at the 3' position of the deoxyribose moiety. In natural nucleosides, this 3'-OH group is essential for forming the 3',5'-phosphodiester bond that links nucleotides together to create the DNA polymer chain. The azido group (-N₃) cannot participate in this phosphodiester bond formation.
- **Chemical Formula and Nomenclature:** The chemical formula of AZT is C₁₀H₁₃N₅O₄. It is also known as zidovudine (ZDV). The systematic chemical name is 3'-azido-2',3'-dideoxythymidine.

**Mechanism of Action**

AZT's antiviral activity stems from its potent and selective inhibition of HIV-1 reverse transcriptase (RT), an enzyme essential for HIV replication.

- **Intracellular Phosphorylation: Activation to ZDV-Triphosphate (ZDV-TP):** AZT itself is a prodrug and requires intracellular activation to its active form, zidovudine triphosphate (ZDV-TP). This activation occurs through a series of sequential phosphorylation steps catalyzed by host cell kinases.
    - **Sequential Phosphorylation:** AZT undergoes three phosphorylation steps within the cytoplasm of cells. Cellular kinases, including thymidine kinase (TK) and nucleoside diphosphate kinase (NDPK), catalyze these phosphorylations.
    - **Formation of ZDV-Monophosphate (ZDV-MP), Diphosphate (ZDV-DP), and Triphosphate (ZDV-TP):** The sequential phosphorylation converts AZT to ZDV-monophosphate, then to ZDV-diphosphate, and finally to the active metabolite, zidovudine triphosphate (ZDV-TP). ZDV-TP is the form that directly inhibits HIV reverse transcriptase. The initial phosphorylation step by thymidine kinase is often rate-limiting and crucial for antiviral activity.
- **Inhibition of HIV Reverse Transcriptase: Chain Termination Mechanism:** ZDV-TP acts as a competitive substrate and a potent inhibitor of HIV reverse transcriptase.
    - **Competition with dTT**P: ZDV-TP structurally mimics the natural substrate deoxythymidine triphosphate (dTTP), which is normally used by reverse transcriptase to synthesize DNA from the viral RNA genome. ZDV-TP competes with dTTP for binding to the active site of HIV reverse transcriptase.
    - **Incorporation into Viral DNA:** HIV reverse transcriptase, lacking the discriminatory ability to perfectly distinguish between ZDV-TP and dTTP, mistakenly incorporates ZDV-TP into the growing viral DNA chain in place of dTTP.
    - **DNA Chain Termination:** Once ZDV-TP is incorporated into the viral DNA, the absence of a 3'-hydroxyl group on the deoxyribose sugar of ZDV-TP prevents the formation of the next 3',5'-phosphodiester bond. This effectively terminates the elongation of the viral DNA chain. The premature termination of viral DNA synthesis halts the reverse transcription process, preventing the integration of viral DNA into the host cell genome and thus blocking viral replication.
- **Selectivity for HIV Reverse Transcriptase: Differential Affinity and Repair Mechanisms:** AZT exhibits significant selectivity for HIV reverse transcriptase over human DNA polymerases (e.g., DNA polymerase α, β, γ).
    - **Higher Affinity for Reverse Transcriptase:** AZT-TP has approximately 100-fold to 300-fold greater affinity for HIV reverse transcriptase compared to human DNA polymerases, particularly DNA polymerase γ (mitochondrial DNA polymerase), but also nuclear DNA polymerases. This differential affinity allows AZT to preferentially inhibit viral DNA synthesis at concentrations that have less impact on host cell DNA replication.
    - **Efficient Human DNA Repair Mechanisms:** Human cells possess efficient DNA repair mechanisms that can recognize and remove AZT-TP incorporated into cellular DNA and replace it with natural nucleotides. HIV, being a virus, lacks such sophisticated DNA repair mechanisms. This difference in DNA repair capacity further contributes to AZT's selective toxicity, allowing human cells to tolerate AZT exposure better than HIV-infected cells.
    - **Mitochondrial Toxicity (DNA Polymerase γ Inhibition):** Despite the selectivity, AZT can still inhibit human DNA polymerase γ, albeit at higher concentrations than required to inhibit HIV reverse transcriptase. Inhibition of mitochondrial DNA polymerase γ can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, contributing to some of the long-term toxicities associated with AZT, such as myopathy and lactic acidosis.

**Applications**

AZT, as the first approved antiretroviral drug, has played a transformative role in the clinical management of HIV/AIDS.

- **HIV Treatment: Component of Combination Antiretroviral Therapy (ART):** AZT is a cornerstone drug in combination antiretroviral therapy (ART) regimens. It is typically used in combination with other antiretroviral agents from different classes (e.g., non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, protease inhibitors, integrase inhibitors) to achieve maximal virologic suppression and prevent the development of drug resistance.
    - **Viral Load Suppression and Immune Function Improvement:** ART regimens containing AZT effectively suppress HIV viral load (the amount of HIV in the blood) to undetectable levels in many patients. Viral suppression allows for the recovery of the immune system, measured by increases in CD4+ T-cell counts, and reduces the risk of opportunistic infections and AIDS-related complications.
    - **Early Stage HIV Infection:** AZT is particularly effective during the early stages of HIV infection when viral replication rates are high.
    - **Historical Significance:** AZT was instrumental in converting HIV infection from a rapidly fatal illness to a chronic, manageable condition. While newer antiretroviral drugs with improved efficacy and tolerability have been developed, AZT remains in use, particularly in resource-limited settings and in specific clinical situations.
- **Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT):** AZT is highly effective in preventing vertical transmission of HIV from pregnant women to their infants.
    - **Prenatal, Intrapartum, and Postpartum Regimens:** AZT is administered to HIV-infected pregnant women during the prenatal period (during pregnancy), intrapartum period (during labor and delivery), and postpartum period (to the infant after birth).
    - **Reduced Transmission Rates:** PMTCT regimens including AZT have dramatically reduced mother-to-child HIV transmission rates from approximately 25-30% without intervention to less than 1% in many developed countries.
    - **Global Impact:** AZT-based PMTCT strategies have had a profound global impact on reducing the incidence of pediatric HIV infection.

**Pharmacokinetics**

AZT exhibits favorable pharmacokinetic properties that support its clinical use.

- **Absorption and Bioavailability:** AZT is rapidly absorbed after oral administration, with peak plasma concentrations typically reached within 0.5 to 1.5 hours. Oral bioavailability is approximately 60-70%, indicating a significant first-pass metabolic effect, although clinically sufficient. Food intake may slightly delay absorption but does not significantly affect overall bioavailability.
- **Distribution: Wide Tissue Distribution and CNS Penetration:** AZT is widely distributed throughout body tissues and fluids. It exhibits good penetration into the central nervous system (CNS), achieving clinically relevant concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), approximately 50-60% of plasma concentrations. CNS penetration is important for treating HIV infection in the brain and preventing or treating HIV-associated neurological complications. AZT also crosses the placenta, which is essential for its use in preventing mother-to-child transmission.
- **Metabolism: Hepatic Glucuronidation and Drug Interactions:** AZT undergoes hepatic metabolism primarily via glucuronidation.
    - **Glucuronidation:** AZT is metabolized in the liver by uridine diphosphate glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) enzymes, mainly UGT2B7, to form an inactive glucuronide metabolite, 5'-glucuronosyl-zidovudine (GAZT).
    - **Drug Interactions:** Drugs that induce or inhibit UGT enzymes can potentially affect AZT metabolism and drug levels. For example, probenecid, an inhibitor of renal tubular secretion, can reduce the renal clearance of GAZT, leading to increased AZT exposure. However, clinically significant drug interactions related to AZT metabolism are generally less common compared to some other antiretroviral classes.
- **Excretion: Renal Elimination and Half-Life:** AZT is primarily eliminated from the body via renal pathways, both as unchanged drug and as its glucuronide metabolite.
    - **Renal Excretion:** Approximately 50-80% of an administered dose is excreted in the urine, with about 40-50% as unchanged AZT and the remainder as GAZT.
    - **Elimination Half-Life:** The elimination half-life of AZT is relatively short, ranging from 1 to 3 hours in individuals with normal renal function. This short half-life necessitates frequent dosing (typically twice daily or three times daily) to maintain therapeutic drug concentrations.
    - **Prolonged Half-Life in Renal Impairment:** In patients with renal impairment, the renal clearance of AZT is reduced, leading to a prolongation of the elimination half-life. In end-stage renal disease, the half-life can be significantly extended.
    - **Dosage Adjustment in Renal Impairment:** Dosage adjustments are essential in patients with renal insufficiency to prevent drug accumulation and toxicity. Dosage reduction or延长 dosing intervals are recommended based on creatinine clearance.

**Side Effects**

While AZT has been a life-saving drug, it is associated with a range of potential side effects, some of which can be serious, particularly with long-term use or at higher doses.

- **Hematological Toxicity: Bone Marrow Suppression:** The most significant and dose-limiting adverse effect of AZT is bone marrow suppression.
    - **Anemia and Neutropenia:** AZT commonly causes anemia (reduction in red blood cell count) and neutropenia (reduction in neutrophil count). Thrombocytopenia (reduction in platelet count) can also occur, although less frequently.
    - **Mechanism: Hematopoietic Progenitor Cell Impact:** AZT can inhibit the proliferation and differentiation of hematopoietic progenitor cells in the bone marrow, leading to decreased production of red blood cells, neutrophils, and platelets. This bone marrow suppression is thought to be related to the inhibition of mitochondrial DNA polymerase γ in hematopoietic cells.
    - **Monitoring and Management:** Regular monitoring of complete blood counts (CBC) is essential during AZT therapy to detect hematological toxicity. Management strategies may include dose reduction, drug discontinuation, or the use of hematopoietic growth factors (e.g., erythropoietin, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor) in severe cases.
- **Gastrointestinal Disturbances:** Gastrointestinal side effects are common, particularly at the initiation of AZT therapy.
    - **Symptoms:** Nausea, vomiting, anorexia (loss of appetite), abdominal discomfort, and diarrhea are frequently reported.
    - **Management:** These gastrointestinal symptoms are often mild to moderate and may resolve with continued therapy. Taking AZT with food may help to reduce nausea. Antiemetics and antidiarrheal medications can be used to manage these symptoms if necessary.
- **Mitochondrial Toxicity: Myopathy and Lactic Acidosis:** Long-term AZT use, especially at higher doses, can lead to mitochondrial toxicity due to the inhibition of mitochondrial DNA polymerase γ.
    - **Mechanism: DNA Polymerase γ Inhibition and Mitochondrial Dysfunction:** AZT can inhibit human mitochondrial DNA polymerase γ, which is essential for the replication and maintenance of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Inhibition of DNA polymerase γ leads to mtDNA depletion and mitochondrial dysfunction.
    - **Myopathy:** Mitochondrial myopathy (muscle disease) is a recognized complication of AZT therapy. Symptoms include muscle weakness, muscle pain (myalgia), and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels. In severe cases, rhabdomyolysis (muscle breakdown) can occur.
    - **Lactic Acidosis:** Mitochondrial dysfunction can impair oxidative phosphorylation and lead to lactic acidosis, a potentially life-threatening condition characterized by the accumulation of lactic acid in the blood. Symptoms of lactic acidosis include fatigue, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, rapid breathing, and altered mental status. Lactic acidosis requires immediate medical attention and AZT discontinuation.
- **Genotoxic Effects and Carcinogenicity Concerns:** AZT has shown evidence of genotoxicity and carcinogenicity in preclinical studies.
    - **Genomic Damage:** *In vitro* and *in vivo* studies have demonstrated that AZT can induce genomic damage, including micronuclei formation (indicators of chromosomal damage) and chromosomal aberrations.
    - **Carcinogenicity in Animal Models:** Long-term carcinogenicity studies in rodents have shown that AZT can increase the incidence of certain tumors, particularly lymphomas and vaginal tumors in female mice and liver tumors in rats.
    - **Clinical Relevance and Risk-Benefit Assessment:** The clinical relevance of these genotoxic and carcinogenic findings in humans is complex and not fully elucidated. While a potential carcinogenic risk cannot be entirely excluded, the significant clinical benefit of AZT in treating HIV infection, which itself increases cancer risk, generally outweighs these theoretical risks in the context of HIV therapy. However, these findings underscore the importance of judicious use and careful risk-benefit assessment, particularly for long-term AZT therapy and in specific patient populations.

## **3.3 Antifungal Drugs**

### **3.3.1 Nystatin**

Nystatin, a prominent polyene macrolide antifungal antibiotic, is marketed under a variety of brand names including Mycostatin, Nilstat, Nyamyc, Nystop, and Nystex. It is naturally produced by the bacterium *Streptomyces noursei*. Nystatin is a cornerstone in the treatment of localized fungal infections, particularly those caused by *Candida* species.

**Structure**

Nystatin's structure is characterized by a large, complex macrocyclic lactone ring, a hallmark of polyene macrolide antifungals.

![Fig. Nystatin Structure](Unit III 1a63a995df8780c3ae22d5b53434e624/image%206.png)

Fig. Nystatin Structure

- **Polyene Macrolide:** Nystatin belongs to the polyene macrolide class, defined by a large lactone ring (a cyclic ester) containing multiple conjugated double bonds (typically four to seven). These conjugated double bonds are responsible for the characteristic UV absorption spectrum and are crucial for antifungal activity, specifically for interacting with ergosterol.
- **Macrocyclic Lactone Ring:** The core structure is a macrocyclic lactone ring, a large ring structure containing an ester linkage (-CO-O-). In nystatin, this ring is complex and contains 38 carbon atoms.
- **Conjugated Double Bonds (Tetraene):** Nystatin contains a system of four conjugated double bonds within the macrolide ring. This tetraene chromophore is responsible for nystatin's characteristic UV-Vis absorption properties and its ability to interact with sterols in fungal membranes. The polyene region is lipophilic and interacts with the lipid environment of the cell membrane.
- **Hydroxyl Groups and Hydrophilicity:** The nystatin molecule is heavily decorated with hydroxyl (-OH) groups along the macrolide ring. These hydroxyl groups impart a degree of hydrophilicity to the molecule, contributing to its amphipathic nature—having both hydrophilic and lipophilic regions.
- **Mycosamine Sugar Moiety:** A key structural feature is the presence of a deoxysugar, D-mycosamine, attached to the macrolide ring via a glycosidic bond. Mycosamine is an amino sugar, containing both a sugar and an amino group. This sugar moiety enhances the molecule's solubility and contributes to its bioactivity and interaction with fungal membranes.
- **Amphotericin B Structural Similarity:** Nystatin is structurally closely related to another important polyene antifungal, amphotericin B. They share the macrocyclic lactone ring, polyene system, and mycosamine sugar, differing in the number of conjugated double bonds (amphotericin B has heptaene, seven double bonds) and some substituents on the macrolide ring. This structural similarity results in a similar mechanism of action targeting ergosterol.
- **Chemical Formula and Molecular Weight:** Nystatin's chemical formula is C47H75NO17, and its molecular weight is approximately 926.1 g/mol, reflecting its large and complex molecular structure.

**Mechanism of Action**

Nystatin's antifungal activity is primarily mediated through its interaction with ergosterol, a vital sterol component of fungal cell membranes. This interaction leads to membrane disruption and cell death.

- **Ergosterol Binding: High Affinity Interaction:** Nystatin's mechanism of action begins with its high-affinity binding to ergosterol in the fungal cell membrane. Ergosterol is an essential sterol in fungal membranes, analogous to cholesterol in mammalian cell membranes, but structurally distinct. The polyene region of nystatin's macrolide ring is critical for this interaction, inserting into the lipid bilayer and associating specifically with ergosterol molecules.
- **Membrane Pore Formation: Channel-Forming Ionophore Activity:** Nystatin is a channel-forming ionophore. Upon binding to ergosterol, multiple nystatin molecules aggregate to form transmembrane channels or pores within the fungal plasma membrane. These pores are aqueous channels that span the lipid bilayer, disrupting the membrane's integrity.
    - **Barrel-Stave Mechanism:** The pore formation is thought to occur via a "barrel-stave" mechanism, where multiple nystatin molecules align in a cylindrical arrangement, with their hydrophilic hydroxyl groups lining the interior of the pore and their lipophilic polyene regions interacting with the lipid environment of the membrane.
- **Increased Membrane Permeability and Leakage of Cellular Contents:** The formation of nystatin-induced pores dramatically increases the permeability of the fungal cell membrane. This increased permeability leads to:
    - **Leakage of Essential Intracellular Constituents:** Small molecules and ions, such as potassium ions (K+), essential metabolites, and other cytoplasmic contents, leak out of the fungal cell through the pores.
    - **Disruption of Electrochemical Gradients:** The uncontrolled efflux of ions disrupts the electrochemical gradients across the plasma membrane, which are crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis, membrane potential, and transport processes.
    - **Influx of Water and Ions:** Simultaneously, there is an influx of water and extracellular ions into the cell, leading to osmotic imbalance and cell swelling.
- **Fungicidal and Fungistatic Activity: Concentration-Dependent Effects:** Nystatin exhibits both fungicidal and fungistatic activity against a broad spectrum of yeasts and yeast-like fungi, particularly *Candida* species.
    - **Fungicidal Action (Cell Killing):** At higher concentrations, the membrane damage caused by nystatin is severe and irreversible, leading to fungal cell death. The extensive leakage of cellular contents and disruption of membrane integrity result in cell lysis and fungicidal effects.
    - **Fungistatic Action (Growth Inhibition):** At lower concentrations, nystatin may primarily exert fungistatic effects, inhibiting fungal growth and replication without necessarily causing immediate cell death. The membrane pores at lower concentrations may be smaller or less numerous, leading to sublethal disruption of membrane function and growth inhibition.
- **Selectivity for Fungal Cells: Ergosterol Specificity:** Nystatin exhibits selective toxicity towards fungal cells with minimal impact on mammalian cells. This selectivity is primarily due to its significantly higher binding affinity for ergosterol, the primary sterol in fungal membranes, compared to cholesterol, the predominant sterol in mammalian cell membranes. While nystatin can weakly interact with cholesterol at very high concentrations, clinically relevant concentrations preferentially target ergosterol-containing fungal membranes. This differential sterol affinity underlies its therapeutic selectivity.
- **Resistance Mechanisms: Limited Resistance in *Candida albicans*:** Resistance to nystatin is relatively uncommon in *Candida albicans*, a major fungal pathogen. However, resistance can develop in other *Candida* species and other fungi, although less frequently than with azole antifungals. Resistance mechanisms are not as well-defined as for other antifungals but may involve:
    - **Reduced Ergosterol Content:** Fungal cells may adapt by reducing the ergosterol content in their membranes, thereby decreasing the target for nystatin.
    - **Modifications in Membrane Lipids:** Alterations in other membrane lipid components may compensate for ergosterol reduction or reduce nystatin's ability to form pores.
    - **Efflux Mechanisms:** Efflux pumps that can actively transport nystatin out of the fungal cell may contribute to resistance, although this is less well-characterized.

**Applications**

Nystatin is primarily used for the treatment and prevention of localized candidiasis due to its poor systemic absorption.

- **Topical and Mucocutaneous Candidiasis:** Nystatin is a mainstay for treating superficial *Candida* infections of the skin and mucous membranes.
    - **Cutaneous Candidiasis (Diaper Rash, Skin Folds):** Topical formulations (creams, ointments, powders) are effective for treating *Candida* skin infections, including diaper rash in infants, intertrigo (infections in skin folds), and paronychia (nail fold infections).
    - **Mucocutaneous Candidiasis:**
        - **Oral Candidiasis (Thrush):** Nystatin oral suspension (swish and swallow or swish and spit) is a common treatment for oral thrush, *Candida* infections of the mouth and oropharynx, frequently seen in infants, immunocompromised individuals, and denture wearers.
        - **Esophageal Candidiasis:** Oral nystatin, although less systemically absorbed, can be used for mild esophageal candidiasis, where the infection is primarily localized to the mucosal surface. For more severe cases, systemically absorbed antifungals are preferred.
        - **Vulvovaginal Candidiasis (Vaginal Yeast Infections):** Nystatin vaginal tablets or suppositories are used for treating vaginal yeast infections caused by *Candida* species.
        - **Anal Candidiasis:** Topical nystatin can be used to treat *Candida* infections around the anal area (perianal candidiasis).
- **Gastrointestinal Candidiasis:** Oral nystatin is effective in treating intestinal candidiasis due to its minimal systemic absorption.
    - **Intestinal *Candida* Overgrowth:** Oral nystatin acts locally within the lumen of the gastrointestinal tract to treat *Candida* overgrowth in the gut lumen. It is used for intestinal candidiasis, although the clinical significance and diagnosis of this condition are sometimes debated.
- **Prophylaxis of Candidiasis:** Nystatin can be used prophylactically to prevent *Candida* infections in high-risk individuals.
    - **Prophylaxis in Immunocompromised Patients:** Oral nystatin suspension is often used as prophylaxis in severely immunocompromised patients, such as those with AIDS with low CD4+ counts, transplant recipients, and patients undergoing intensive chemotherapy, to prevent oral and esophageal candidiasis.
    - **Prophylaxis During Antibiotic Therapy:** Nystatin oral suspension may be used prophylactically in patients receiving broad-spectrum antibiotics to prevent oral or intestinal *Candida* overgrowth that can result from antibiotic-induced disruption of normal bacterial flora.
    - **Prevention of Invasive Candidiasis in Neonates (Off-Label):** In some neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), oral nystatin is used off-label for the prevention of invasive candidiasis in very low birth weight neonates, who are at high risk for systemic fungal infections.
- **Combination Products:** Nystatin is available in combination formulations for treating mixed infections.
    - **Nystatin with Antibacterial and Anti-inflammatory Agents:** Combination products containing nystatin with antibacterial agents (e.g., neomycin, gramicidin D) and a corticosteroid (e.g., triamcinolone) are used to treat corticosteroid-responsive dermatoses complicated by bacterial or candidal infections, and for conditions like pruritus ani/vulvae where mixed infections are suspected.
    - **Nystatin with Metronidazole:** Combination vaginal preparations of nystatin and metronidazole are available for treating mixed vaginal infections caused by *Trichomonas vaginalis* and *Candida albicans*.

**Pharmacokinetics**

Nystatin is characterized by its extremely poor systemic absorption, which dictates its clinical applications and safety profile.

- **Minimal Systemic Absorption:** Systemic absorption of nystatin following oral, topical, or vaginal administration is negligible. After oral administration, nystatin remains primarily in the gastrointestinal tract. Topical and vaginal applications result in no detectable systemic absorption.
    - **Undetectable Plasma Concentrations:** Due to minimal absorption, nystatin does not achieve detectable concentrations in plasma after topical, vaginal, or oral administration at recommended doses. This lack of systemic absorption limits its use to localized infections but also minimizes systemic toxicity.
- **Distribution: Local Action:** As nystatin is not systemically absorbed, it does not distribute to body tissues or fluids in significant amounts. Its action is confined to the site of application:
    - **Gastrointestinal Tract (Oral):** Oral nystatin acts locally within the lumen of the gastrointestinal tract.
    - **Skin and Mucous Membranes (Topical):** Topical formulations act locally on the skin and mucous membranes where applied.
    - **Vagina (Vaginal):** Vaginal suppositories and tablets act locally within the vaginal mucosa.
- **Metabolism: Not Systemically Metabolized:** Because nystatin is not systemically absorbed, it does not undergo significant systemic metabolism.
- **Excretion: Fecal Excretion (Oral Administration):** Orally administered nystatin is primarily excreted in the feces, as it passes through the gastrointestinal tract without being absorbed. Topically and vaginally applied nystatin is eliminated locally through natural shedding processes of the skin and vaginal mucosa.
    - **Lack of Renal Excretion:** Due to the absence of systemic absorption, renal excretion is not a significant route of elimination for nystatin.

**Side Effects**

Nystatin is generally considered very safe, with a favorable side-effect profile, primarily due to its negligible systemic absorption.

- **Local Reactions (Topical Use):** Topical application of nystatin is generally well-tolerated, but mild local reactions can occur in some individuals.
    - **Skin Irritation:** Mild skin irritation, redness, burning sensation, stinging, or itching at the application site may occur, particularly with topical formulations.
    - **Hypersensitivity Reactions (Rare):** Allergic contact dermatitis or other hypersensitivity reactions to nystatin or components of the topical formulation are rare but possible.
- **Gastrointestinal Disturbances (Oral Use):** Oral nystatin suspension or tablets can cause gastrointestinal side effects in some patients, although these are usually mild and transient.
    - **Nausea and Vomiting:** Nausea and vomiting are the most commonly reported gastrointestinal side effects with oral nystatin.
    - **Diarrhea:** Diarrhea may occur in some individuals.
    - **Poor Taste:** Nystatin oral suspension has a known poor taste, which can be unpleasant for some patients, especially children, and may contribute to nausea. Taste masking strategies are sometimes employed in formulations to improve palatability.
- **Systemic Toxicity: Minimal Due to Poor Absorption:** Due to its lack of systemic absorption, nystatin is virtually devoid of systemic toxicity. Systemic side effects are extremely rare. This excellent safety profile makes nystatin a preferred agent for treating localized fungal infections, especially in vulnerable populations such as infants, pregnant women, and individuals with liver or kidney disease, where systemic drug exposure is undesirable.
- **Descriptive Safety Assessments:** Descriptive safety assessments and post-marketing surveillance data consistently show that nystatin is well-tolerated. Poor taste and mild gastrointestinal upset are the most commonly reported adverse effects, while serious adverse events are exceedingly rare.

### **3.3.2 Griseofulvin**

Griseofulvin is a fungistatic antifungal agent, a polyketide derivative naturally produced by the mold *Penicillium griseofulvum*. It is specifically effective against dermatophyte fungi and is primarily used to treat superficial fungal infections of the skin, hair, and nails.

**Structure**

Griseofulvin's structure is characterized by a unique spirocyclic system and a benzofuran moiety.

![Fig. Griseofulvin](Unit III 1a63a995df8780c3ae22d5b53434e624/image%207.png)

Fig. Griseofulvin

- **Polyketide Structure:** Griseofulvin is a polyketide, a class of natural products biosynthesized from acetate-derived units. Polyketides often exhibit complex and diverse structures and biological activities.
- **Spirocyclic System:** The core of griseofulvin's structure is a spirocyclic arrangement. A spirocyclic compound is one in which two rings are joined at a single atom (the spiroatom). In griseofulvin, this spiroatom is a quaternary carbon atom that is part of both a benzofuran ring and a cyclohexanone ring. This spirocyclic feature is distinctive and important for its biological activity.
- **Benzofuran Moiety:** Griseofulvin contains a benzofuran ring system, which is a bicyclic structure consisting of a benzene ring fused to a furan ring (a five-membered ring with one oxygen atom). The benzofuran moiety is planar and aromatic.
- **Methoxy Groups:** Griseofulvin is substituted with multiple methoxy (-OCH₃) groups at various positions around its ring system. These methoxy groups influence its lipophilicity and pharmacokinetic properties. Specifically, it has methoxy groups at positions 2, 4, and 6.
- **Chlorine Atom at 7-Position:** A chlorine atom (Cl) is present at the 7-position of the benzofuran ring. This chlorine substituent is crucial for griseofulvin's antifungal activity. Analogs lacking the chlorine atom are significantly less active. The chlorine atom likely contributes to the molecule's electronic properties and interaction with its target.
- **Poor Water Solubility:** Griseofulvin is poorly soluble in water, which affects its absorption and formulation. It is more soluble in organic solvents like ethanol and methanol. Formulations of griseofulvin are often designed to enhance its absorption despite its poor water solubility, such as microsized and ultramicrosized formulations.
- **Chemical Formula and Molecular Weight:** Griseofulvin's chemical formula is C17H17ClO6, and its molecular weight is approximately 352.8 g/mol.

**Biosynthesis**

Griseofulvin biosynthesis is a complex process involving polyketide biosynthesis pathways in *Penicillium griseofulvum*.

- **Polyketide Synthase (PKS) Pathway:** Griseofulvin is assembled through a type I iterative polyketide synthase (PKS) pathway. Type I PKSs are large, multi-domain enzymes that catalyze the stepwise condensation of acyl-CoA units to build polyketide chains. Iterative PKSs use the same set of catalytic domains repeatedly to extend and modify the growing polyketide chain.
    - **Acetyl-CoA and Malonyl-CoA Units:** The biosynthesis begins with the condensation of acetyl-CoA (a starter unit) and malonyl-CoA (extender units). Malonyl-CoA units are added sequentially to the growing polyketide chain.
    - **14-Carbon Poly-β-keto Chain Formation:** Iterative condensation reactions by the PKS enzyme result in the formation of a linear 14-carbon poly-β-keto chain. This chain is still attached to the PKS enzyme.
- **Cyclization and Aromatization: Benzophenone Intermediate Formation:** The linear polyketide chain undergoes cyclization and aromatization reactions while still enzyme-bound.
    - **Benzophenone Formation:** Cyclization and aromatization lead to the formation of a benzophenone intermediate. Benzophenones are molecules containing a ketone group (=CO) attached to two benzene rings. This benzophenone intermediate is a crucial precursor in griseofulvin biosynthesis, establishing the basic ring system.
- **Methylation, Halogenation, and Oxidation:** Subsequent enzymatic modifications introduce specific substituents onto the benzophenone intermediate.
    - **Methylation:** Methyltransferases catalyze the addition of methoxy (-OCH₃) groups at specific positions on the benzophenone ring. These methylation steps are essential for achieving the final griseofulvin structure and its biological activity.
    - **Halogenation: Chlorination at 7-Position:** A halogenase enzyme catalyzes the chlorination reaction, introducing the chlorine atom at the 7-position of the benzofuran ring. This chlorination step is critical for antifungal activity.
    - **Oxidation:** Oxidation reactions introduce hydroxyl or keto groups at specific positions, further modifying the benzophenone intermediate.
- **Spirocyclic Structure Formation: Radical Coupling and Stereoselective Reduction:** The final steps involve complex enzymatic transformations to form the characteristic spirocyclic system of griseofulvin.
    - **Radical Coupling:** A radical coupling reaction is thought to be involved in forming the spirocyclic linkage. Radical coupling involves the joining of two molecules through the formation of a new carbon-carbon bond via radical intermediates.
    - **Stereoselective Reduction:** A stereoselective reductase enzyme catalyzes a reduction reaction that establishes the correct stereochemistry at the spiro center, yielding the final griseofulvin product with the desired stereoconfiguration.

**Mechanism of Action**

Griseofulvin is a fungistatic agent, meaning it inhibits fungal growth rather than directly killing fungal cells. Its primary target is the microtubule system in fungal cells, and it is specifically active against dermatophyte fungi.

- **Inhibition of Mitosis: Microtubule Disruption:** Griseofulvin's primary mechanism of action is the disruption of fungal mitosis (cell division). It achieves this by interfering with microtubule function.
    - **Tubulin Binding:** Griseofulvin binds to fungal tubulin, specifically β-tubulin, a protein subunit that is essential for the assembly of microtubules. Microtubules are dynamic polymers of tubulin that form the mitotic spindle, a structure crucial for chromosome segregation during cell division.
    - **Microtubule Assembly Disruption:** Griseofulvin binding to tubulin inhibits the polymerization of tubulin subunits into microtubules and disrupts the dynamic instability of microtubules. It prevents proper microtubule formation and organization.
    - **Mitotic Spindle Dysfunction:** Disruption of microtubule assembly and function leads to mitotic spindle dysfunction. The mitotic spindle is unable to properly separate chromosomes during mitosis.
    - **Cell Cycle Arrest at Metaphase:** As a result of mitotic spindle dysfunction, fungal cell division is arrested at the metaphase stage. Metaphase is the stage of mitosis where chromosomes are aligned at the metaphase plate, ready to be separated. Griseofulvin-induced metaphase arrest prevents further progression through the cell cycle and inhibits fungal proliferation.
- **Keratin Binding: Incorporation into Keratinized Tissues:** A unique aspect of griseofulvin's action is its ability to bind to keratin, the structural protein of skin, hair, and nails.
    - **Binding to Keratin Precursor Cells:** Griseofulvin is actively transported into keratin precursor cells (keratinocytes) in the skin, hair follicles, and nail beds. It binds to newly synthesized keratin as these cells differentiate and become keratinized.
    - **Fungistatic Protection of New Keratin:** By binding to keratin, griseofulvin renders the newly formed keratin resistant to fungal invasion. The drug is incorporated into the keratinized tissues as they grow out, creating a barrier against dermatophyte infection.
    - **Replacement of Infected Tissue:** Griseofulvin treatment does not directly eradicate existing fungal infections within already keratinized tissue. Instead, it prevents new keratin from being infected. The infected keratinized tissue is gradually shed and replaced by uninfected, griseofulvin-containing keratin over time as hair, skin, and nails grow. This process of replacement of infected tissue with uninfected tissue is central to its therapeutic efficacy.
- **Energy-Dependent Uptake into Fungal Cells:** Griseofulvin entry into fungal cells is not by simple diffusion but involves energy-dependent transport mechanisms.
    - **Active Transport System:** Fungal cells possess an active transport system that facilitates the uptake of griseofulvin across the cell membrane. This energy-dependent uptake ensures that griseofulvin reaches therapeutic concentrations within fungal cells to exert its microtubule-disrupting effects.
    - **Specificity for Fungal Cells:** The energy-dependent uptake system may contribute to some degree of selectivity for fungal cells, although griseofulvin's selectivity is primarily based on the differences in tubulin between fungi and mammals.

**Applications**

Griseofulvin's clinical use is primarily focused on the treatment of dermatophyte infections, particularly those affecting keratinized tissues.

- **Dermatophyte Infections (Tinea Infections):** Griseofulvin is specifically indicated for the treatment of tinea infections (ringworm) caused by dermatophyte fungi, including *Trichophyton*, *Microsporum*, and *Epidermophyton* species.
    - **Tinea Capitis (Scalp Ringworm):** Griseofulvin is highly effective for tinea capitis, a common dermatophyte infection of the scalp and hair, particularly in children. Oral griseofulvin is often the treatment of choice for tinea capitis due to its ability to penetrate hair follicles and treat the infection at the hair root.
    - **Tinea Corporis (Ringworm of the Body):** Griseofulvin is used to treat tinea corporis, ringworm infections of the trunk, limbs, and face. However, topical antifungals are often preferred for localized tinea corporis due to fewer side effects. Oral griseofulvin may be used for extensive or refractory cases.
    - **Tinea Cruris (Jock Itch):** Griseofulvin can be used for tinea cruris, dermatophyte infections of the groin area. Topical antifungals are often first-line, but oral griseofulvin may be considered for widespread or resistant infections.
    - **Tinea Pedis (Athlete's Foot):** Griseofulvin is effective against tinea pedis (athlete's foot), dermatophyte infections of the feet. However, topical antifungals are generally preferred for tinea pedis due to their direct application to the affected area and lower risk of systemic side effects. Oral griseofulvin may be used for severe or chronic cases, or nail involvement.
    - **Tinea Unguium (Onychomycosis - Nail Fungus):** Griseofulvin is used to treat onychomycosis, fungal infections of the fingernails and toenails. However, treatment of onychomycosis with griseofulvin is prolonged (months to years), and newer antifungal agents, such as terbinafine, itraconazole, and fluconazole, are often preferred due to higher efficacy, shorter treatment durations, and broader spectra of activity. Griseofulvin is generally less effective against onychomycosis compared to these newer agents.
- **Prophylaxis of Dermatophyte Infections (Limited Role):** While griseofulvin is not routinely used for prophylaxis, it may be considered in specific situations.
    - **Prophylaxis in High-Risk Individuals (Rarely Used):** In individuals at very high risk for recurrent or severe dermatophyte infections, such as those with certain immunosuppressive conditions or living in communal settings with high rates of tinea capitis, prophylactic griseofulvin *might* be considered in very specific circumstances under specialist guidance. However, prophylactic use is not common due to the availability of effective topical treatments and the potential for side effects with prolonged oral griseofulvin. Antifungal hygiene measures and topical antifungals are generally preferred for prophylaxis.

**Pharmacokinetics**

Griseofulvin's pharmacokinetics are characterized by variable absorption and deposition in keratinized tissues.

- **Oral Administration and Variable Absorption:** Griseofulvin is administered orally. Absorption from the gastrointestinal tract is variable and incomplete, influenced by formulation and food intake.
    - **Microsized vs. Ultramicrosized Formulations:** Griseofulvin is available in microsized and ultramicrosized formulations. Ultramicrosized formulations have significantly improved bioavailability compared to microsized formulations due to smaller particle size and enhanced dissolution and absorption. Ultramicrosized griseofulvin achieves approximately 1.5-2 times higher serum concentrations than microsized formulations at the same dose.
    - **Food Effect: Enhanced Absorption with Fatty Foods:** Absorption of griseofulvin is significantly enhanced when administered with fatty foods. Fatty meals increase griseofulvin absorption, likely by promoting dissolution and lymphatic transport. Patients are typically advised to take griseofulvin with or after a fatty meal to maximize absorption.
- **Distribution: Keratin-Rich Tissues and Systemic Distribution:** Griseofulvin is widely distributed in body tissues, with a particular affinity for keratin-rich tissues.
    - **Selective Deposition in Keratinized Tissues:** Griseofulvin concentrates and deposits in keratin precursor cells and keratinized tissues, including skin, hair, and nails. Concentrations in keratinized tissues can be several times higher than in plasma. This selective deposition is crucial for its efficacy against dermatophyte infections of these tissues.
    - **Systemic Distribution:** While preferentially deposited in keratinized tissues, griseofulvin also distributes systemically to other tissues and fluids, although concentrations in non-keratinized tissues are lower.
- **Metabolism: Hepatic Metabolism and Drug Interactions:** Griseofulvin undergoes extensive hepatic metabolism, primarily via cytochrome P450 enzymes.
    - **Hepatic Metabolism:** Griseofulvin is metabolized in the liver by cytochrome P450 enzymes, mainly CYP1A2, CYP2C9, and CYP3A4. Metabolism involves demethylation and glucuronidation, leading to inactive metabolites.
    - **CYP450 Enzyme Induction:** Griseofulvin is an inducer of hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes, particularly CYP1A2, CYP2C9, and CYP3A4. Enzyme induction can lead to drug interactions by increasing the metabolism and reducing the serum concentrations of co-administered drugs that are substrates of these enzymes.
    - **Drug Interactions:** Griseofulvin can interact with various drugs metabolized by CYP450 enzymes, including warfarin (reduced anticoagulant effect), oral contraceptives (reduced contraceptive efficacy), cyclosporine, and barbiturates. Dosage adjustments of co-administered drugs may be necessary.
- **Excretion: Renal and Fecal Excretion:** Griseofulvin and its metabolites are eliminated via both renal and fecal routes.
    - **Renal Excretion:** A portion of griseofulvin metabolites is excreted in the urine.
    - **Fecal Excretion:** The major route of excretion is via feces, primarily as metabolites. Unabsorbed griseofulvin is also excreted in feces.
    - **Elimination Half-Life:** The elimination half-life of griseofulvin is variable, typically ranging from 9 to 24 hours, depending on the formulation and individual patient factors.

**Side Effects**

Griseofulvin is generally considered to have a reasonable safety profile, but it can cause a range of side effects, some of which are dose-dependent and can be bothersome.

- **Gastrointestinal Disturbances:** Gastrointestinal side effects are common with oral griseofulvin.
    - **Symptoms:** Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, epigastric distress, and flatulence are frequently reported.
    - **Management:** Taking griseofulvin with food, particularly fatty foods (to enhance absorption), may paradoxically help to reduce gastrointestinal upset in some patients. Antiemetics and antidiarrheal medications can be used if symptoms are significant.
- **Central Nervous System (CNS) Effects:** CNS side effects are relatively common.
    - **Headache:** Headache is a frequently reported side effect.
    - **Dizziness and Vertigo:** Dizziness, lightheadedness, and vertigo (a sensation of spinning) can occur.
    - **Fatigue and Lethargy:** Fatigue, tiredness, and lethargy are common.
    - **Insomnia:** Difficulty sleeping (insomnia) may occur.
    - **Confusion and Impaired Concentration:** In some individuals, particularly at higher doses or in elderly patients, confusion, impaired concentration, and memory problems can occur.
- **Allergic Reactions and Skin Rashes:** Hypersensitivity reactions, including skin rashes, can occur.
    - **Skin Rashes:** Various types of skin rashes, including urticaria (hives), erythematous rashes, and morbilliform eruptions, have been reported.
    - **Photosensitivity:** Griseofulvin can cause photosensitivity, increasing the risk of sunburn upon exposure to sunlight or UV radiation. Patients should be advised to take sun protection measures.
    - **Rare Severe Reactions:** Rarely, more severe allergic reactions, such as angioedema (swelling of the face, lips, tongue, throat) and Stevens-Johnson syndrome, have been reported.
- **Hematological Effects (Rare):** Hematological side effects are uncommon but can occur.
    - **Leukopenia:** Reduction in white blood cell count (leukopenia) has been reported, which can increase the risk of infection.
    - **Neutropenia:** Specifically, a decrease in neutrophils (neutropenia) may occur.
    - **Thrombocytopenia:** Reduction in platelet count (thrombocytopenia), increasing the risk of bleeding, is rare.
    - **Monitoring Blood Counts:** Periodic monitoring of complete blood counts (CBC) may be recommended during prolonged griseofulvin therapy, especially in patients at risk for hematological abnormalities.
- **Hepatotoxicity (Rare):** Hepatic toxicity is rare but has been reported with griseofulvin.
    - **Elevated Liver Enzymes:** Transient elevations in liver enzymes (transaminases) may occur.
    - **Hepatitis (Rare):** Hepatitis and liver damage are rare but serious potential side effects.
    - **Liver Function Monitoring:** Liver function tests (LFTs) may be monitored in patients receiving prolonged griseofulvin therapy, especially those with pre-existing liver disease.
- **Drug Interactions:** Due to its cytochrome P450 enzyme induction, griseofulvin has the potential for numerous drug interactions. Clinically significant interactions include reduced efficacy of oral contraceptives and reduced anticoagulant effect of warfarin, requiring dosage adjustments of these co-administered medications.

Despite its potential side effects and drug interactions, griseofulvin remains a valuable antifungal agent, particularly for tinea capitis and other dermatophyte infections, especially when newer agents are not suitable or accessible. However, for onychomycosis and less extensive dermatophyte infections, newer antifungals with improved efficacy and safety profiles are often preferred.

## **3.4 Mechanism of Action of Other Therapeutic Agents**

### **3.4.1 Anticancer Drugs**

Anticancer drugs, also known as chemotherapy agents, are pharmacological substances designed to treat malignant diseases by inhibiting the growth and proliferation of cancer cells. These drugs operate through various mechanisms, targeting specific cellular processes essential for cancer cell survival and replication. The primary goals of anticancer therapies include damaging the DNA of cancer cells, inhibiting the synthesis of new DNA strands, and disrupting cell division.

![Fig. Structure of some Anticancer Drugs.](Unit III 1a63a995df8780c3ae22d5b53434e624/image%208.png)

Fig. Structure of some Anticancer Drugs.

**Classes of Anticancer Drugs and Their Mechanisms:**

1. **Alkylating Agents:** These drugs introduce alkyl groups into DNA, leading to cross-linking and strand breaks. This disrupts DNA structure and function, ultimately resulting in cell death. These agents react with nucleophilic sites on DNA bases, forming adducts that interfere with DNA replication and transcription.
2. **Antimetabolites:** Antimetabolites are structural analogs of normal molecular components essential for cell growth. They interfere with nucleotide synthesis, preventing cancer cells from replicating.
    - **Mechanism:** These agents inhibit enzymes involved in DNA synthesis or become incorporated into DNA, causing chain termination. Examples include methotrexate, which inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, blocking folate metabolism necessary for purine and pyrimidine synthesis, and fluorouracil, which inhibits thymidylate synthase, disrupting the formation of thymidine needed for DNA synthesis.
3. **Topoisomerase Inhibitors:** These medications target topoisomerases, enzymes that regulate DNA topology by introducing single-strand breaks.
    - **Class I Inhibitors (Topoisomerase I Poisons):** These drugs stabilize the cleavable complex between topoisomerase I and DNA, preventing religation and leading to DNA breaks. An example is camptothecin.
    - **Class II Inhibitors:** These agents target topoisomerase II, causing double-strand breaks in DNA. Some drugs that target topo II are DNA intercalators: acridines, anthracyclines, actinomycin, and ellipticines.
4. **Mitotic Disruptors:** These drugs disrupt the formation of mitotic spindles, which are essential for separating newly copied DNA during cell division. These drugs work by attacking malignant cells and interfering with cell division. Examples include vinblastine, vincristine, and paclitaxel. These bind to tubulin and inhibit the formation of microtubules, causing metaphase arrest.
5. **L-Asparaginase:** This enzyme destroys asparagine external to the cell. Lymphatic tumor cells require large amounts of asparagine to sustain their rapid growth. L-asparaginase converts asparagine in the blood into aspartic acid by a deamination reaction, depriving leukemia cells of their supply of asparagine, leading to cell death.
6. **Targeted Therapies:**
    - **Kinase Inhibitors:** Some anticancer drugs target specific kinases involved in cancer cell growth and survival. Imatinib and dasatinib are medications used to treat Philadelphia chromosome-positive CML and acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL) by targeting aberrant BCR-ABL kinase. Imatinib also inhibits other tyrosine kinases (TKs), such as c-KIT and PDGFR, making it useful for treating other malignancies such as gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs). Similarly, dasatinib inhibits other TKs, such as PDGFR and EphA, and is being investigated for its potential use in various other malignancies.

**Mechanisms of Resistance:**

- **Changes in Drug Transport:** Changes in membrane transport are a common mechanism whereby cancerous cells become resistant to chemotherapeutic drugs. To exert their cytotoxic effects, many anti-cancer drugs must undergo metabolic activation[1](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2013.00028/full). For instance, cytarabine, necessitates initial phosphorylation by deoxycytidine kinase to cytarabine-monophosphate which is subsequently phosphorylated to the active form cytarabine triphosphate.
- **Inactivation of Anticancer Drugs:** Cancer cells become resistant by reducing the activity of drugs. GST enzymes increase the drug resistance in cancer cells directly by the detoxification of anti-cancer drug or indirectly by the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway inhibition in the RAS-MAPK path.
- **Changing the Drug Metabolism:** Chemotherapeutic agent metabolisms can be occurred by enzymes. These reactions reduce the drug resistance in the cancer cells via two manners including 1. reducing the activation of pro-drugs (reduced the activity of some enzymes) and 2. increasing the drug inactivation (increased activity of some enzymes).
- **Enhance DNA Repair:** The resistance to anticancer agents occurs by the DNA repair systems, including nucleotide excision repair system (NER) and homologous recombination repair mechanisms (RRM) in the cancer cells.

**Other Mechanisms:**

- Rational treatments that target RAS pathways have the potential to inhibit the growth and spread of tumors.
- Some natural products exert antitumor effects by targeting the endoplasmic reticulum stress apoptosis pathway.

Understanding the diverse mechanisms of action employed by anticancer drugs is essential for developing new therapeutic strategies and improving treatment outcomes.

### **3.4.2 Antidiabetic Drugs**

Antidiabetic drugs are a diverse group of medications used to manage diabetes mellitus, primarily type 2 diabetes. These agents work through various mechanisms to regulate blood glucose levels, improve insulin sensitivity, and enhance insulin secretion. The main classes of antidiabetic drugs include:

![Fig. Antidiabetic Drugs](Unit III 1a63a995df8780c3ae22d5b53434e624/image%209.png)

Fig. Antidiabetic Drugs

1. **Biguanides:**
    - **Example:** Metformin
    - **Mechanism of Action:** Metformin primarily improves insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues, particularly muscle and adipose tissue. It decreases hepatic glucose production by inhibiting gluconeogenesis and reduces intestinal absorption of glucose. Additionally, metformin activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which plays a crucial role in cellular energy homeostasis, leading to enhanced glucose uptake and utilization by cells.
2. **Sulfonylureas:**
    - **Examples:** Glibenclamide, Glipizide, Gliclazide
    - **Mechanism of Action:** Sulfonylureas stimulate the pancreas to secrete more insulin by binding to the sulfonylurea receptor (SUR) on pancreatic beta cells. This action closes ATP-sensitive potassium channels, causing depolarization of the cell membrane and opening voltage-gated calcium channels. The influx of calcium ions triggers the exocytosis of insulin granules from the beta cells into the bloodstream.
3. **Dipeptidyl Peptidase-4 (DPP-4) Inhibitors:**
    - **Examples:** Sitagliptin, Saxagliptin
    - **Mechanism of Action:** DPP-4 inhibitors prevent the breakdown of incretin hormones, such as GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1). By increasing the levels of these hormones, DPP-4 inhibitors enhance insulin secretion in response to meals and suppress glucagon release from alpha cells in the pancreas. This results in reduced hepatic glucose production and improved glycemic control.
4. **Thiazolidinediones (TZDs):**
    - **Examples:** Pioglitazone, Rosiglitazone
    - **Mechanism of Action:** TZDs act as agonists for peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ), a transcription factor that regulates genes involved in glucose and lipid metabolism. Activation of PPAR-γ enhances insulin sensitivity in adipose tissue and skeletal muscle, promotes fatty acid storage in adipocytes, and reduces circulating free fatty acid levels. This leads to improved glucose uptake and decreased hepatic glucose production.
5. **Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) Inhibitors:**
    - **Examples:** Canagliflozin, Dapagliflozin
    - **Mechanism of Action:** SGLT2 inhibitors block the reabsorption of glucose in the renal proximal tubules by inhibiting the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2. This action leads to increased urinary excretion of glucose, thereby lowering blood glucose levels independently of insulin.
6. **Alpha-Glucosidase Inhibitors:**
    - **Examples:** Acarbose, Miglitol
    - **Mechanism of Action:** These agents inhibit alpha-glucosidase enzymes in the intestinal brush border, which are responsible for breaking down complex carbohydrates into simple sugars. By delaying carbohydrate digestion and absorption, alpha-glucosidase inhibitors reduce postprandial blood glucose spikes.
7. **Amylin Analogues:**
    - **Example:** Pramlintide
    - **Mechanism of Action:** Amylin analogues mimic the action of amylin, a hormone co-secreted with insulin by pancreatic beta cells. They slow gastric emptying, decrease postprandial glucagon secretion, and promote satiety, leading to reduced food intake and improved glycemic control.

**Combination Therapy:**

In many cases, antidiabetic medications are used in combination to achieve better glycemic control. For instance, metformin is often combined with sulfonylureas or DPP-4 inhibitors to enhance overall efficacy while minimizing side effects.

### **3.4.3 Antihypertensive Drugs**

Antihypertensive drugs are essential in the management of hypertension, a condition characterized by persistently elevated blood pressure that can lead to serious cardiovascular complications. These medications work through various mechanisms to lower blood pressure and improve overall cardiovascular health. The main classes of antihypertensive drugs include:

![Fig. Chemical structures of hypertensive drugs used in the method validation](Unit III 1a63a995df8780c3ae22d5b53434e624/image%2010.png)

Fig. Chemical structures of hypertensive drugs used in the method validation

1. **Diuretics:**
    
    Diuretics are often the first-line treatment for hypertension as they reduce blood volume by promoting the excretion of sodium and water through the kidneys.
    
    - **Thiazide Diuretics:** These agents inhibit the Na+/Cl- co-transporter in the distal convoluted tubule of the nephron, leading to increased excretion of sodium and chloride, which in turn promotes diuresis. Thiazides also have a long-term effect on lowering blood pressure through vasodilation, although the exact mechanism is not fully understood.
    - **Loop Diuretics:** These are more potent than thiazides and act on the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, inhibiting the Na+/K+/2Cl- co-transporter. This results in significant diuresis and is particularly useful in patients with heart failure or renal impairment.
    - **Potassium-Sparing Diuretics:** These agents act primarily in the collecting ducts and are used to prevent potassium loss that can occur with other diuretics. They either antagonize aldosterone or inhibit sodium channels directly, leading to mild diuresis while retaining potassium.
2. **Beta-Blockers:**
    
    Beta-blockers reduce blood pressure by blocking beta-adrenergic receptors, which are activated by catecholamines (epinephrine and norepinephrine).
    
    - **Mechanism:** By blocking beta-1 receptors in the heart, these drugs decrease heart rate and myocardial contractility, leading to reduced cardiac output. Additionally, they inhibit renin release from the kidneys, which decreases angiotensin II formation and promotes vasodilation.
3. **Alpha-1 Blockers:**
    
    Alpha-1 blockers selectively block alpha-1 adrenergic receptors located on vascular smooth muscle.
    
    - **Mechanism:** This blockade prevents vasoconstriction caused by sympathetic stimulation, resulting in vasodilation and decreased peripheral vascular resistance.
4. **Central Sympatholytics:**
    
    Central sympatholytics act on the central nervous system to reduce sympathetic outflow.
    
    - **Mechanism:** By stimulating alpha-2 adrenergic receptors in the brainstem, these agents decrease sympathetic tone, leading to reduced heart rate and vasodilation.
5. **ACE Inhibitors:**
    
    Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors block the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II, a potent vasoconstrictor.
    
    - **Mechanism:** By reducing levels of angiotensin II, these drugs decrease systemic vascular resistance and promote renal excretion of sodium and water. Additionally, they prevent the breakdown of bradykinin, which contributes to their vasodilatory effects.
6. **Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs):**
    
    ARBs inhibit the action of angiotensin II at its receptor sites.
    
    - **Mechanism:** By preventing angiotensin II from binding to its receptors, ARBs effectively reduce vasoconstriction and promote renal excretion of sodium and water.
7. **Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs):**
    
    Calcium channel blockers inhibit calcium entry into vascular smooth muscle cells and cardiac myocytes.
    
    - **Mechanism:** By blocking L-type calcium channels, these drugs cause relaxation of vascular smooth muscle (vasodilation) and decrease cardiac contractility, leading to a reduction in blood pressure.
8. **Vasodilators:**
    
    Vasodilators directly relax vascular smooth muscle.
    
    - **Mechanism:** These agents may work through various pathways, including nitric oxide release or direct action on smooth muscle cells. They are typically used for acute management of severe hypertension or hypertensive emergencies.
9. **Renin Inhibitors:**
    
    Renin inhibitors target the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) by inhibiting renin's activity.
    
    - **Mechanism:** By blocking renin, these drugs prevent the formation of angiotensin I from angiotensinogen, thereby reducing levels of angiotensin II and subsequent vasoconstriction.
10. **Aldosterone Receptor Antagonists:**
    
    Aldosterone receptor antagonists block the effects of aldosterone on its receptor in the kidneys.
    
    - **Mechanism:** This blockade reduces sodium reabsorption in exchange for potassium retention, leading to increased diuresis and decreased blood pressure.
11. **Alpha-2 Adrenergic Agonists:**
    
    Alpha-2 agonists stimulate receptors in the central nervous system that lead to decreased sympathetic outflow.
    
    - **Mechanism:** By activating these receptors, they reduce norepinephrine release from sympathetic neurons, resulting in lowered heart rate and vascular resistance.
